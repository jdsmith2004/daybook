After a slip

How do I stop one slip from turning into a binge?

Step out right where you are. One slip does not commit you to the rest of the night, or the rest of the week. The risky moment is the thought that comes right after it: “I already blew it, so it does not matter now.”

The “I already blew it” thought

Psychologists Marlatt and Gordon named this the abstinence violation effect: after a slip, giving up feels like the logical next step. You may know its other voice too: “I am back at day one anyway, so why stop now?” That thought only works if the slip erased everything, and it did not. A slip is one event, and you can step out of the snowball right there.

In a study of people quitting smoking, those whose confidence dropped after a lapse tended to lapse again sooner. Protecting your confidence is what stops the second slip.

Leave the setting

A slip usually happens in a setting that keeps pulling: the same bed, the same phone, the same hour. Habits lean on their setting, so the quickest way out is to leave it. Get up, turn on a light, go to another room. Moving helps the body too: a short bout of exercise eases cravings for a while.

Notice what it really gave you

The picture before a slip is bright and loud. The real thing is usually smaller than it promised. Noticing that, with curiosity rather than shame, wears the pull down. In a trial with 88 adult smokers, people trained to notice their cravings with curiosity did far better than people in a standard quit program: four months later, 31% were not smoking, against 6%. Ask yourself honestly: what did I expect it to give, and what did it really give?

One thing to do tonight

  • Put the phone in another room, now.
  • Get up: a glass of water, a light on, a short walk through the house.
  • Write down what happened in one honest line, then go to sleep. Tomorrow, start your day as usual.
