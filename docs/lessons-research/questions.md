# Lesson questions: what the evidence says, and what a good question looks like for behavior change

Research for Daybook, 2026-11-01. Nothing in the repository was edited.

## How to read this file

- Every number below was read in a source during this run. Where I read the paper itself (as a PDF) I say "primary". Where I only saw an abstract, a search snippet or someone else's summary I say "secondary" and the number should be checked before it goes in any public text.
- Evidence strength uses the scale from the brief: Strong (several RCTs or a meta-analysis), Moderate (a few RCTs or good longitudinal), Weak (pilot or cross-sectional), Expert view only. I add one honest split that matters a lot here: most of the learning research is about **remembering words and facts in a lab or classroom**. Almost none of it measures **what a person does at 11pm**. So each rule below carries two grades where they differ: "for learning" and "for behavior".
- Things I looked for and did NOT find are listed at the end. Do not treat a gap as support.

---

## The short answer

1. The owner is right, and the research says why. A question whose right answer is obvious tests nothing, so it teaches nothing. The testing effect and the pretesting effect both need real uncertainty (the person has to try to produce or choose between things they might get wrong) and then true feedback. "Walk outside or watch porn" has no uncertainty, no plausible second option, and no feedback worth reading.
2. The strongest findings about questions (retrieval practice, spacing, generation) are about memory for facts. They are Strong for that. They are weak evidence for changing 11pm behavior, because an intention is not an action: a medium-to-large change in intention produced only a small-to-medium change in behavior (Webb & Sheeran 2006).
3. For behavior, the best-supported question types make the person (a) commit to a guess or choice, (b) say their own reason or plan in their own words, (c) form a specific if-then plan, and (d) see their own data. Those have Moderate-to-Strong evidence across health behaviors, though not for porn specifically.
4. Questions with one acceptable answer risk the opposite of what is wanted. In motivational interviewing research, a confrontational style raised client resistance and resistance predicted worse drinking outcomes a year later (Miller, Benefield & Tonigan 1993, small RCT). A question that is really an instruction in disguise is the in-app version of that.
5. Removing the start-of-lesson review question loses a small, real thing (spaced reuse of yesterday's idea). That can be kept in a form that does not feel like a quiz. See "If the review question is removed".

## 1. Retrieval practice, the testing effect, desirable difficulty, spacing

### What the evidence says

- **Rowland (2014), Psychological Bulletin, meta-analysis, PRIMARY (I read the PDF).** Testing vs restudy. Overall effect g = 0.50 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.58), k = 159 effect sizes. Findings that matter for design:
  - Initial test type: cued recall g = 0.61, free recall g = 0.29, recognition g = 0.29 in the full set. In a "high-exposure" subset that controls for feedback and initial success, recall (0.72 cued, 0.81 free) was larger than recognition (0.36).
  - Feedback: with feedback g = 0.73; without feedback g = 0.39.
  - Success matters when there is no feedback: with no feedback and 50% or lower initial success, g = 0.03 (no benefit); at 51 to 75% success g = 0.29; above 75% success g = 0.56.
  - Retention interval: 1 day or longer g = 0.69; under 1 day g = 0.41. Testing helps most for what you need to know tomorrow.
  - Author's reading: supports "effortful processing" accounts. Recognition tests still give a reliable benefit, just smaller.
  - https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559 (PDF read via https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
  - Strength: Strong, for memory of verbal material.
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), Review of Educational Research 87(3), 659-701, meta-analysis. SECONDARY ONLY** (the publisher page returned 403; I saw the abstract and summaries). Reported: practice tests beat restudy and all other comparison conditions; 118 articles, 272 independent effect sizes, 15,472 participants (secondary summary); moderators include test features. One secondary summary says multiple-choice was the most effective single format and one full-length test beat several in a short window; another (Learning Scientists blog) says hybrid multiple-choice plus cued recall was best and feedback added only slightly. These two summaries disagree, so I do not rely on either. The blog's title says 217 studies; the 118/272 figures appear in more than one source.
  - https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306 ; https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1
  - Strength: Strong (meta-analysis), details unverified.
- **Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt (2021), Educational Psychology Review. SECONDARY ONLY.** Systematic review of retrieval practice in real classrooms: 50 experiments, about 49 effect sizes, 57% medium or large benefit (per project page summary). Benefit across formats and timing. https://www.retrievalpractice.org/review . Strength: Moderate to Strong for classrooms.
- **Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 4-58. SECONDARY (ratings reported on several pages).** Practice testing and distributed practice rated high utility; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving rated moderate utility. Practice testing with corrective feedback gave the highest rates of learning. The authors note elaborative interrogation and self-explanation were under-tested in real classrooms. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266 . Strength: expert synthesis of Strong evidence (for learning).
- **Desirable difficulties (Bjork).** Bjork & Bjork (2011), "Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way", in Psychology and the Real World, Worth, pp. 56-64. Secondary summary from search results plus the lab's PDF listing. Key point from the authors' own framing: difficulty helps only when it triggers useful encoding and retrieval processes; many difficulties are undesirable. So "harder" is not the goal; "effortful but doable" is. https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf . Strength: Strong theory-plus-experiments for learning; expert view for design translation.
- **Spacing: Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), Psychological Bulletin 132(3), 354-380.** 839 assessments in 317 experiments in 184 articles; spacing beats massing, and the best gap grows with the retention interval (abstract, via PubMed; I did not read the full text). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/ . **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), Psychological Science 19(11), 1095-1102.** More than 1,350 people, gaps up to 3.5 months, tests up to a year; the best gap was roughly 20% of the delay for delays of a few weeks, falling to about 5% at one year (secondary summary; the "10 to 20%" rule of thumb is NOT what I found). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/ . Strength: Strong (for verbal recall).

### Direct answers to the questions asked

- **How strong?** Strong for remembering studied material (g about 0.5 overall in Rowland). Not shown for behavior.
- **Does it need to be effortful?** Mostly yes. Recall beat recognition in Rowland. But the key moderator is a pairing: effort plus success or feedback. Low success with no feedback gave about zero benefit (g = 0.03).
- **Does it work with multiple choice?** Yes, if the options are competitive (Little & Bjork, below) and feedback follows. Smith & Karpicke (2014, Memory 22, 784-802, abstract and secondary summary of the PDF at https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Smith_Karpicke_Memory.pdf) found across three experiments little or no advantage of short-answer or hybrid over multiple choice, and an advantage only when short-answer retrieval success was raised. Strength: Moderate.
- **Does an easy question help?** For memory, a question the person answers correctly still gives a benefit (Rowland: above 75% success, no feedback, g = 0.56). So "easy" is not harmless-but-useless in the lab. The problem with "walk outside or watch porn" is different: nothing is being retrieved because there is nothing to remember and no real alternative to weigh. It is neither retrieval nor decision. The testing-effect benefit attaches to material the person has to hold in memory; it does not attach to a question whose answer is a moral given.
- **Does recall at the start of a lesson help, and is there a better form?** Spaced retrieval of yesterday's idea fits the research (a one-day gap, and Rowland's larger effect at 1 day or more). A better form for this app is to bring back the **action or plan** rather than a fact, see "If the review question is removed" below.
- **Which recall forms help without being a quiz?** Free recall in the person's own words ("what is the one thing from yesterday you would tell a friend?"), recall embedded inside a new scenario, and reuse of a plan the person wrote. All of these are retrieval; none looks like a quiz. Evidence that these specific forms work in an app: none found; the evidence is for retrieval in general (Moderate for learning, Expert view for the app form).

## 2. Multiple-choice quality

- **Little, Bjork, Bjork & Angello (2012), Psychological Science 23(11).** Properly built multiple-choice tests with plausible wrong options trigger retrieval of why the right answer is right and why the others are wrong, and helped recall of information tied to the wrong options; cued-recall tests did not (abstract and secondary coverage). https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/10/121005123902.htm . Strength: Moderate (two experiments).
- **Little & Bjork (2014), "Optimizing multiple-choice tests as tools for learning", Memory & Cognition. PRIMARY (I read the PDF).** Answering questions with **competitive** wrong options improved later cued recall for tested items and for **related, untested** information, specifically information tied to the previous wrong options. This did NOT happen when the wrong options were **noncompetitive**. Replicated in two experiments, with and without feedback. The mechanism proposed: with competitive options the person retrieves information about all of the options to discriminate. https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2017/01/LittleBjorkMC2014.pdf . Strength: Moderate.
  - This is the direct evidence for the owner's complaint: obvious distractors are noncompetitive, so they produced no benefit at all in this study.
- **Butler & Roediger (2008), Memory & Cognition 36(3), 604-616. SECONDARY.** Multiple-choice testing can plant false knowledge from lures; feedback after the choice nullified that negative effect. https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.3.604 . Strength: Moderate. Design consequence: feedback after every choice, especially when an option is a tempting wrong one.
- **Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez (2002), Applied Measurement in Education 15(3), 309-333. SECONDARY (listing and summaries).** A review of item-writing guidelines; few guidelines have been tested empirically. **Rodriguez (2005), Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice 24(2), 3-13, meta-analysis. SECONDARY.** Three options are generally best provided each wrong option is plausible; extra options that nobody picks add nothing. https://cpl.health.unm.edu/AssetListing/Writing-Questions-for-Learning-and-Assessment-2573/Rules-for-Multiple-Choice-Items-Haladyna-et-al-2002-5943 . Strength: Moderate for "plausible options only, three is often enough"; the rest of the rulebook is Expert view (consolidated). I did not retrieve the specific "non-functioning distractor" threshold papers.
- **Why obvious distractors teach nothing (reasoning, anchored in the above).** If the person can pick the answer without retrieving anything, the item is a recognition by elimination. Little & Bjork showed the benefit comes from discriminating between competitive options. A cue like "the healthy option is also the longest and most moral" lets people answer by test-wise guessing, which is the "fishing" the owner describes.
- **Can a good question have no single right answer?** Yes, but not as an exam item. In assessment the key must be defensible. For behavior change, a question can have no single correct option when it asks about a **trade-off, a preference, or a plan**. Situational judgment tests handle disagreement by using consensus or expert keys, and have useful validity for interpersonal job performance (McDaniel et al. 2001 rho about .34, secondary; Webster et al. 2020 meta-analysis, pooled r .26 cited, secondary: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/159850/). That is for selection, not teaching, so it only shows that scenario items with judged answers are a mature format. For Daybook, drop the key: give consequences for each option instead of a grade (Expert view).

## 3. Pretesting and guess first, hypercorrection, generation, self-explanation

- **Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009), J Exp Psychol: Applied 15(3), 243-257. PRIMARY (read the PDF).** Five experiments. Being asked about concepts **before** reading an essay produced better later performance than extended study, even counting only items answered wrongly on the pretest. Experiment 5 is the useful one for an app: on posttest keyword questions, test-and-study M = 90%, **seeing the questions first without answering** M = 78%, extended study M = 63% (d = 1.1 for the first vs the last; d = 0.57 for the middle vs extended study). Items that were neither tested nor emphasized did not differ. https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf . Strength: Moderate (several lab experiments).
- **Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), JEP:LMC 35(4), 989-998. ABSTRACT + secondary.** Failed attempts to answer, even for questions almost impossible to answer, improved later learning versus reading the answer. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/ . Strength: Moderate.
- **Pan & Sana (2021), JEP: Applied 27(2), 237-257. SECONDARY.** Compares pretesting and posttesting; results mixed across studies. https://pure.athabascau.ca/en/publications/pretesting-versus-posttesting-comparing-the-pedagogical-benefits-/ . I did not find a definitive pretesting meta-analysis in this run.
- **Sinha & Kapur (2021), Review of Educational Research (productive failure). SECONDARY (press coverage and an unofficial mirror).** Meta-analysis of problem-solving-then-instruction vs instruction-then-problem-solving, reported as 53 studies and 166 comparisons; the press multiplier ("two to three times") should not be quoted. Reported limit: it helps conceptual learning more than procedures. https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/ ; DOI 10.3102/00346543211019105 . Strength: Moderate, details unverified.
- **Hypercorrection: Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001), JEP:LMC 27(6), 1491-1494. SECONDARY.** High-confidence errors are more likely to be corrected after feedback than low-confidence errors, attributed to the surprise of the mismatch. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercorrection_(psychology) (summary only). Strength: Moderate for facts. Not tested for beliefs about oneself or for urges.
- **Generation effect.** Slamecka & Graf (1978), J Exp Psychol: Human Learning and Memory 4(6), 592-604 (excerpt PDF at https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/Slamecka1978.pdf ): self-generated items were remembered better than read items. **Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott & McDaniel (2007), Memory & Cognition 35(2), 201-210, meta-analysis (abstract only):** 445 effect sizes, 86 studies, mean about 0.40. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/ . Strength: Strong (for words).
- **Self-explanation.** Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994), Cognitive Science 18(3), 439-477: 14 eighth-graders; prompted to self-explain gained more than controls who reread (n very small). https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-de-leeuw-n-chiu-m-h-lavancher-c-1994-eliciting-self-explanations . **Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018), Educational Psychology Review, meta-analysis (secondary summary):** overall g = .55, 69 effect sizes from 64 reports; prompt types g = .56 (interrogative, 36 studies), .40 (imperative, 12), .70 (pre-directed, 8). https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf ; https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/CHU-KR-25_ENG_Bisra_2018_Selbsterklaerungen.pdf . Strength: Strong (students, mostly undergraduates). Caveat from the review: mostly undergraduates and academic material.
- **Elaborative interrogation ("why is this true?")** rated moderate utility by Dunlosky 2013 (above). Note: asking "why" about an obviously true statement is the same fishing problem. It works on claims the learner can reason about.
- **Prediction questions.** Evidence is the pretesting and productive-failure work above, plus Richland Exp. 5. For an app, a slider guess is a one-tap pretest. Strength: Moderate for facts.
- **One caution from the research on all of these.** Every "guess first" benefit in the studies depended on the person getting the true answer afterwards. A guess with no reveal has no evidence behind it.

## 4. Transfer to real behavior

- **The gap between intending and doing.** Webb & Sheeran (2006), Psychological Bulletin 132(2), 249-268, meta-analysis of 47 experimental tests: a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) led to a small-to-medium change in behavior (d = 0.36) (figures from secondary summaries). https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/1580 . Strength: Strong. Implication: a lesson that only changes what the person believes or intends will underdeliver at 11pm.
- **Implementation intentions (if-then plans).** Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38, 69-119: 94 independent tests, d = 0.65 on goal attainment (secondary summary), mostly against a goal-intention-only control. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 . Later, Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer, "The when and how of planning", European Review of Social Psychology 36 (2025), 162-194: 642 tests, d from .27 to .66 depending on outcome; plans did better when explicit if-then, when motivation was high, and when rehearsed (secondary summary). https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/69905 . A blog claims a bias-corrected estimate of d = 0.15; I could not verify it, so treat the true effect as smaller than 0.65. Strength: Strong that it helps; moderate for the size.
- **Writing vs reading a plan.** I found NO source that tests writing a plan against reading one. Rehearsal is reported as a moderator (secondary). Strength: Expert view only for "write it, do not read it".
- **Mental contrasting with implementation intentions (WOOP, Oettingen).** Cross & Sheffield (2019), Health Psychology Review: mental contrasting for health behavior, MCII g = 0.28 (CI 0.14 to 0.42, k = 7), no extra benefit from adding implementation intentions (secondary). https://repository.derby.ac.uk/item/93z0q/mental-contrasting-for-health-behaviour-change-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-effects-and-moderator-variables . Wang, Wang & Gai (2021), Frontiers in Psychology: 21 studies, 24 effect sizes, N = 15,907, g = 0.336 for goal attainment, with publication bias concerns (secondary). https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full . Strength: Moderate (small effects).
- **Knowledge vs skill practice.** Tobler et al. (2000), school drug prevention: interactive programs gave a larger effect than non-interactive ones (0.17 vs 0.05 for the smoking subset, as reported in a National Academies chapter; secondary) and an earlier meta-analysis found the same direction. https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11795/chapter/15 . Strength: Moderate (adolescent school programs). Consistent with "information alone does not change use".
- **Coping skills and relapse prevention.** Irvin, Bowers, Dunn & Wang (1999), J Consulting and Clinical Psychology 67(4), 563-570: 26 studies, 70 tests, N = 9,504; relapse prevention generally effective, particularly for alcohol (abstract; I did not retrieve effect sizes). https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.67.4.563 . Magill & Ray (2009), cognitive-behavioral treatment for alcohol and drug users, 53 controlled trials, small effect (g about .15; sources differ between .144 and .163). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2696292 . Strength: Moderate that skills training works; the effect is small, which is an honest limit on what any app lesson can claim.
- **Behavioral skills training (instruction, modeling, rehearsal, feedback).** I only found recent reviews in autism and staff training settings, one of which says the four components still lack rigorous component testing. I did not find evidence for BST delivered by an app. Strength: Weak for this use; Expert view that rehearsal plus feedback are the active parts.
- **Mental rehearsal in general.** A well-known meta-analysis exists (Driskell, Copper & Moran 1994, J Applied Psychology) but I did not retrieve it this run, and the only page that cited a 35-study figure was a low-quality blog, so I give no number.
- **Scenario-based learning / situational judgment.** I found validity data for SJTs in selection (above) and no meta-analysis of scenario-based training effectiveness. Strength: Weak for teaching use.
- **Porn-specific digital evidence (honest picture).** Crosby & Twohig (2016), in-person ACT for problematic pornography use, RCT with 28 men, 93% reduction in viewing vs 21% for waitlist (https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/psych_facpub/1355). Bőthe et al. (2021), "Hands-off", web self-help, 264 randomized, lower problematic use at follow-up but 89.4% dropout in the intervention arm vs 44.7% control, 34.5% overall follow-up, so low confidence (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8987418). Baumeister et al. (2024), J Behavioral Addictions, imaginal retraining: per-protocol effect, intention-to-treat did not corroborate, retention 51.7% (secondary). Strength: Weak.
- **What it takes for reading to change 11pm behavior (synthesis, my judgment from the above).** (1) A specific plan in if-then form tied to the person's own cue (late evening, tired, phone in bed). (2) Having rehearsed saying or doing it, not only read it. (3) Having seen, in their own record, that the cue is real. (4) A reason in their own words that they can recall under urge. (5) Repetition across days (spacing). That is what the six question kinds below are built to do. Expert view, assembled from Moderate-to-Strong parts.
- **Identity framing.** Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck (2011), PNAS 108(31), 12653-12656: framing voting as "being a voter" instead of "voting" raised turnout (for example 81.8% to 95.5% in one statewide election; 79.0% to 89.9% in another); a replication attempt prompted the authors to say context matters. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3150938 . Strength: Moderate/Weak. Relevant to "someone who is clean". Caution: the effect was on a valued, positive identity; noun framing of a stigmatized label ("addict") is untested here and the app already avoids it.

## 5. Motivational interviewing, evocation, reactance, self-persuasion

- **MI basics (Miller & Rollnick).** Open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries; evoke the person's own reasons ("change talk"); avoid the "righting reflex" (the urge to fix and correct). I did not read the book this run and cite no page. The research tests below are what I could read.
- **Miller & Rose (2009), American Psychologist 64(6), 527-537.** Proposes a theory with a relational component (empathy, spirit) and a technical component (differentially evoking and reinforcing change talk). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2759607/ . Strength: Expert view / theory with supporting studies.
- **Miller, Benefield & Tonigan (1993), J Consulting and Clinical Psychology 61, 455-461. ABSTRACT (secondary summary).** 42 problem drinkers randomized to a confrontational or client-centered brief counseling style, or waitlist. The confrontational style produced more client resistance, and resistance predicted poorer outcomes at one year; more confrontation correlated with more drinking (r = .65). Both styles reduced drinking vs control. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8326047/ . Strength: Moderate (small RCT).
- **Amrhein, Miller, Yahne, Palmer & Fulcher (2003), J Consulting and Clinical Psychology 71, 862-878.** 84 drug users; strength of the client's commitment language late in the session predicted drug use outcomes; later studies replicate only partly. https://motivationalinterviewing.org/content/client-commitment-language-during-motivational-interviewing-predicts-drug-use-outcomes . Strength: Moderate (longitudinal, partial replication).
- **Magill et al. meta-analyses of client language (about 2018 to 2019).** From search snippets only: more sustain talk goes with less behavior change; change talk alone is a weak predictor. I did not verify author-year-journal details, so cite only as "the MI process meta-analyses" until checked. Strength: Moderate.
- **Reactance.** Rains (2013), Human Communication Research 39(1), 47-73: meta-analysis of 20 studies (N = 4,942) of reactance to persuasive messages, finding that anger and counterarguing mark reactance (secondary). https://academic.oup.com/hcr/article-abstract/39/1/47/4093682 . Strength: Moderate. Mapping this to lesson questions: a question whose only acceptable answer is the "healthy" one is a persuasive message with the person's agreement demanded. It invites counter-arguing and the quiet decision to click through. This mapping is my inference, not a tested result.
- **Self-persuasion.** Aronson, Fried & Stone (1991), American Journal of Public Health: mindfulness of past condom failures plus advocating condom use raised stated intentions (PMC1405296 listed; secondary). Stone et al. (1994), PSPB 20, 116-128: in the hypocrisy condition more people bought condoms (over 80% vs 30 to 50% in other conditions, secondary summary). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167294201012 . Strength: Moderate (lab experiments, with a behavioral measure in 1994). **Design caution:** the effective ingredient there was making the person aware of past failure. In Daybook that is shame, which the app forbids. Use the advocacy half only ("what would you say to a friend in this exact spot").
- **Digital MI-style prompts.** The reviews I found cover technology-delivered adaptations of MI (Pedamallu et al. 2022 scoping review, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9399886/) and a remote-MI meta-analysis in chronic disease (Baricchi et al. 2023, depression SMD -0.20, no HbA1c effect; judged inconclusive). I found no trial isolating a single MI-style prompt inside a self-guided app. Strength: Weak.
- **Decisional balance (hold both sides).** Hall & Rossi (2008), Preventive Medicine 46(3), 266-274: across 48 behaviors, moving from not considering change to taking action goes with about +1 SD in the pros and -0.5 SD in the cons (cross-sectional) (abstract). https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/psy_facpubs/663 . Strength: Weak to Moderate (cross-sectional). It supports asking about both pros and cons, not that doing so causes change.

## 6. Using the person's own record

- **Tailoring in general.** Noar, Benac & Harris (2007), Psychological Bulletin 133(4), 673-693: 57 studies, N = 58,454, weighted mean r = .074 (small but reliable); moderators include comparison type, behavior, number of contacts and what was tailored on. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17592961/ . Strength: Strong that tailoring helps; the size is small. **Lustria et al. (2013)**, Journal of Health Communication 18(9), 1039-1069: 40 studies, N = 20,180; tailored web interventions d = .14 at post-test and .16 at follow-up, from a secondary citation, and an erratum exists (not checked). **Krebs, Prochaska & Rossi (2010)**, Preventive Medicine 51, 214-221: 88 computer-tailored interventions, effects meaningful for smoking, activity, diet and screening, and **dynamic tailoring** (re-tailored over time) held up better than single-assessment tailoring (abstract). https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/psy_facpubs/428 . Strength: Strong for small effects.
- **Self-monitoring.** Michie, Abraham, Whittington, McAteer & Gupta (2009), Health Psychology 28(6), 690-701: 101 studies; self-monitoring plus at least one control-theory technique gave 0.42 vs 0.26 without (abstract and CRD summary). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/ . **Harkin et al. (2016)**, Psychological Bulletin 142(2), 198-229: 138 randomized studies, N = 19,951; prompting monitoring raised monitoring (d+ = 1.98) and goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, CI .32 to .48); effects larger when progress was physically recorded (about .43 vs .29, secondary) or reported (.47 vs .19, secondary). https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/ . Strength: Strong. Most studies were weight, smoking, diet. Daybook's log fits "physically recorded".
- **Personalized feedback.** A meta-analysis of stand-alone personalized normative feedback for college drinkers (8 studies, 13 interventions, 2,050 participants) found greater reductions than controls (title and summary as returned: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4598082/ ; author attribution not verified). Carey et al. (2009), Addiction 104, 1807-1819: computer-delivered interventions beat assessment-only but rarely beat alcohol-relevant comparison content (abstract). Strength: Moderate.
- **Asking about future behavior changes behavior.** Wood, Conner, Miles, Sandberg, Taylor, Godin & Sheeran (2016), Personality and Social Psychology Review 20(3), 245-268: 116 published tests, small positive effect (d+ = 0.24); larger for more desirable, less risky, easier behaviors and in student samples; little support for the standard explanations (secondary: summary via the commentary page https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4899924/). A separate review (Lawton et al. 2016) reported a smaller pooled effect (g = 0.14), and another (Spangenberg et al. 2016) found support for several mechanisms. Strength: Moderate (small). **Design consequence (my inference):** ask about the wanted act ("what will you do at 10:00") and not "how likely is a slip tonight". The reviews found the effect is larger for desirable behaviors; I did not verify what happens when the question names an undesirable act. So avoid it.
- **Honest limits.** Tailoring effects are small (r about .07). Personalization is a multiplier on good content, not a cure for bad content. A question that quotes the person's log but asks something obvious still fails.

## 7. How other apps build in-lesson questions (only what I could verify)

I could verify very little. App companies publish few design details, and most pages found were reviews or store listings.

- **Brilliant.** Own words ("Why Brilliant" help page): "active problem solving, visual models, guided feedback", and feedback that "explains why an answer was wrong instead of just marking it wrong". App Store listing claims interactive learning is "6x more effective than watching lecture videos"; I found no study behind this, so treat it as marketing. A third-party review describes lessons as sequences of interactive problems, each starting with a short concept intro of 2 to 4 sentences. I did not find Brilliant's own description of how lessons are authored. https://brilliant.org/help/why-brilliant ; https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id913335252
- **Duolingo.** Primary: Settles & Meeder (2016), "A Trainable Spaced Repetition Model for Language Learning", ACL 2016, 1848-1858; half-life regression predicts when a learner will forget an item; error about half that of the Leitner baseline; Settles says it produced a 12% engagement boost in production (interview, secondary). https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174 (listed as https://preview.aclanthology.org/landing_page/P16-1174). I did not find a public description of how Duolingo chooses exercise types. What to take: it schedules reuse by estimated forgetting, not by showing a "review" screen.
- **Noom.** Reviews (not research): short daily articles and quizzes, 16 weeks of lessons in courses, an onboarding quiz that builds a profile; one reviewer criticizes that previous lessons are hard to review. https://ncoa.org/adviser/healthy-living/noom-review ; https://www.medicspot.co.uk/weight-loss/programmes/noom . Noom's question-writing process: not found.
- **Reframe.** Reviews: a daily short reading, a 120-day program, craving diversions, tracking, community, subscription. Company-claimed outcomes only (91% reduced drinking, a company claim). The search returned nothing on porn or on a quiz format. https://www.healthline.com/health/mental-health/reframe-app-review
- **Headspace.** I found trial registrations and small pilots, and nothing on how its questions are built.
- **What to copy (my judgment, not tested):** (a) Brilliant's habit of putting a problem first and making the feedback explain why the tempting wrong answer is tempting (matches Little & Bjork and Butler & Roediger). (b) Duolingo's idea that reuse of old material is scheduled quietly, not announced as a quiz. (c) Noom's use of a short profile at the start to personalize (matches tailoring evidence, which says dynamic re-tailoring is better; Daybook's log can do that). What not to copy: Noom-style paywall funnels, and any "streak" mechanics, which conflict with the owner's rules.

## A plain rubric for this app

Eight rules. The two grades are "for learning" and "for behavior".

1. **Ask only what the person cannot already answer, or what only they can answer.** If the right answer is obvious, delete the question. A question is worth asking when there is a real guess, a real trade-off, or a fact about their own life.
   - Learning: Strong (retrieval and pretesting need uncertainty and feedback; Rowland 2014, Richland et al. 2009). Behavior: Expert view only.
2. **Make every option something a real person would pick at 11pm, each with an honest reason.** No straw options. Two or three options are enough.
   - Learning: Moderate (Little & Bjork 2014; Rodriguez 2005 secondary). Behavior: Expert view only.
3. **Guess first, then show the true answer.** Use the slider or a quick choice before the reveal; the reveal must be real.
   - Learning: Moderate (Richland 2009; Kornell 2009; Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001 secondary). Behavior: Weak (nothing tested on urges).
4. **Feedback always follows, explains the cost and the pull of each option, and never marks the person wrong.**
   - Learning: Strong (Rowland: feedback g = 0.73 vs 0.39; Butler & Roediger 2008). Behavior: Expert view only.
5. **Make the person say a reason, a plan or a sentence in their own words.** Open or semi-open, never a trap. Ask for the advice they would give a friend, not a confession.
   - Learning: Strong (generation d about .40, self-explanation g about .55). Behavior: Moderate (commitment language predicts outcomes, observational; Amrhein 2003; Stone 1994 in the lab).
6. **Use the person's own record whenever one exists.** Ask what they would change, not whether the data is true.
   - Learning: n/a. Behavior: Moderate to Strong (self-monitoring d+ = .40, tailoring r about .07, dynamic tailoring more durable).
7. **End on a rehearsed if-then move tied to their own cue.** A short written "When **_, I will _**", not a general intention.
   - Learning: n/a. Behavior: Strong that if-then plans help (d about .65, likely smaller after bias; Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006). Writing vs reading: Expert view only. Intention-to-behavior gap is real: Webb & Sheeran 2006 Strong.
8. **Do not demand one acceptable answer on questions about values or choices. Show consequences, not a verdict. Never ask anything that works as a confession test or invites a "gotcha".**
   - Learning: Weak. Behavior: Moderate (Miller et al. 1993 small RCT; Rains 2013 reactance meta-analysis; mapping to quiz questions is my inference).

Do not spend lesson questions on facts the person has no use for. If a fact will not change what they do, it is lesson text, not a question.

## Six kinds of good question (made-up examples, in the app's voice)

These examples are illustrations of the form. Any number in a reveal must come from the lesson's own sourced material; I marked those spots.

### 1. The hard trade-off (both answers are defensible)

> It is 10:30 at night. A friend texts and asks you to come over. Going means a late night and a tired morning. Staying home means a quiet evening with your phone. Which one is the better risk tonight?
>
> - Go over.
> - Stay home.
> - It depends. Tell me what it depends on.

Feedback: yes, but no grade. After the pick, show both costs in plain words: "Going costs sleep and gives you company. Staying home costs company and gives you rest. What decides it is which risk you can plan for." Then ask one follow-up in the person's own words: "What would you set up so that your choice goes well?" Why it works: both options are real (Rule 2, Rule 8), the plan question makes them generate (Rule 5).

### 2. The guess about yourself

> Think about the last week. At what hour is it hardest for you?
>
> - Before 8 pm.
> - Between 8 and 10.
> - After 10.
> - I do not know yet.

Feedback: none on correctness. If the person has a log, show the log next to the guess without a verdict: "Your guess: after 10. Your log: 4 of 6 urges between 9 and 11." If the log is empty, say "Your log will show this in a week" and invite one-tap logging. Why: self-monitoring and own-record (Rule 6); "I do not know yet" is an honest choice, so nobody is pushed to fake an answer. Never mark a guess as right or wrong.

### 3. The prediction before the fact

> Before you read on: an urge arrives and you do nothing but wait, breathe and keep your hands busy. How many minutes before it starts to fall?
> [slider, 0 to 60 minutes]

Feedback: yes. Reveal the true figure from the lesson ([figure and source go here]) and one sentence: "Most people guess longer than this. You can outlast it." The slider guess is a one-tap pretest (Rule 3). The reveal must be real and sourced; no reveal means no benefit.

### 4. The own-log question

> Your log says most of your hard moments come after 10 pm. What is one small thing that could be different at 9:45?

Open text, with three of the person's own earlier answers offered as chips if they have any. Feedback: no grade. The answer is saved and returned later as part of the plan ("Last week you said you would put the phone on the kitchen counter"). Why: dynamic tailoring (Krebs 2010), commitment language (Amrhein 2003), advice-to-self. It only works when the log line is true and specific; if the data is thin, ask kind 2 instead.

### 5. The rehearsal (write the sentence you will say)

> Write the sentence you will say to yourself when you reach for your phone in bed tonight.
> Start with "When I...".

Feedback: a format nudge only, no judgment of content. If the sentence lacks a cue or an action, show: "Good start. Add the moment ('when I lie down') and your move ('I put the phone on the counter')." Then bring the sentence back on Today in the evening and in the SOS tool. Why: if-then planning (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006), rehearsal as a moderator (Sheeran et al. 2025, secondary). Note: writing vs reading is untested; this is a design bet.

### 6. Choose your move with a named consequence

> It is 11 pm. The urge is at a 7. Your phone is in your hand. What do you do?
>
> - Stand up and drink a glass of water in the kitchen.
> - Stay in bed and wait it out with the phone face down.
> - Open a distracting app until it passes.

Feedback: yes, per option, immediately, without "wrong". For each: "Kitchen: you are away from the bed, the screen and the cue. The urge may still climb for a few minutes. It passes without you feeding it." "Phone face down in bed: you stay near the cue, so the next ten minutes are harder." "Distracting app: the screen stays in your hand, so the same device and the same pull stay close." Every option has a true cost and a true gain (Rule 2), so people pick by reasoning, and the consequence text does the teaching (Rule 4). The consequence lines above are illustrative and must be backed by the lesson's content.

### Which should give feedback, and how, without grading honesty

- **Factual reveal (kind 3, and kind 6's consequences): yes, immediate, factual.** This is where feedback has the strongest evidence (Rowland: 0.73 vs 0.39).
- **About the person (kinds 2, 4, 5): show information, never a verdict.** Put the guess and the log side by side. Say "your log says" and stop. Do not say "that is not right" or "be honest". Offer "I do not know yet" and "I would rather not say" on every question about the self.
- **Trade-offs (kind 1): show both costs, then ask for the plan.**
- **Rehearsal (kind 5): format help only.**
- Never ask a question whose only function is to catch someone saying what they think they should say. If the app already knows the answer from the log, show it instead of asking.

## If the review question is removed

**What is lost:**

- A spaced retrieval of yesterday's idea. That is a real, replicated effect: spacing (Cepeda 2006, 317 experiments) and retrieval (Rowland 2014; effect larger at 1 day or more, g = 0.69). Strength: Strong for facts.
- A bridge between lessons (continuity). Expert view.
- A small chance to catch a misunderstanding. Weak.

**What is NOT lost:** the benefit is for material the person needs to hold. In a habit app, what has to stick is not lesson facts. It is a few things: their own reason, their own if-then plan, and their own cue. A quiz about the lesson does not retrieve those.

**Is there a form that keeps the benefit and does not feel like a quiz?** Yes. Options, in my order of preference (Expert view on the app form; the retrieval principle itself is Strong):

1. **Return the person's own plan and ask how it went.** "Yesterday you wrote: 'When I lie down, I put the phone on the counter.' Did you get to use it?" Answers: used it / not yet / forgot. This is retrieval (they must recall the plan), self-monitoring (Harkin 2016), and it asks about an action, not a fact. No grade.
2. **Reuse yesterday's idea inside today's scenario.** The scenario needs the earlier idea to choose well, with no label. That is spaced retrieval hidden in application.
3. **One free-recall prompt in the person's own words.** "What is the one thing from yesterday you would tell a friend?" Free recall had a bigger effect than recognition in Rowland's controlled set, and a text box has no right answer.
4. **Duolingo-style quiet scheduling.** Resurface a past idea as a line on Today when the person's own record suggests it matters (for example, a late-evening pattern), instead of a review card.

My view: remove the start-of-lesson multiple-choice recall as the owner wants; replace it with option 1 or 2. That keeps what the evidence supports and drops what the owner dislikes. Where an old idea must come back as a question, show the answer after (feedback) so a failed recall does not leave nothing (Rowland: low success without feedback gave about zero benefit).

## What I looked for and could not verify (do not cite these as support)

- Adesope et al. (2017): effect sizes (g) and the multiple-choice and feedback moderators. I have the study counts only through secondary sources, and the two secondary sources disagree on feedback and best format.
- Haladyna's specific item-writing rules and the non-functioning distractor thresholds.
- Magill et al. MI process meta-analyses: author, year and journal need checking.
- Behavioral skills training and mental rehearsal meta-analyses.
- Any study comparing writing to reading an implementation intention.
- Any trial of an app that uses MI-style prompts in self-guided lessons on its own.
- Any published account of how Brilliant, Noom, Reframe or Headspace write their in-lesson questions.
- A pretesting meta-analysis (I found single studies and a productive-failure meta-analysis only through press coverage).
- Evidence that asking about an undesirable behavior ("how likely is a slip?") raises or lowers it. The reviews I saw only say the effect is larger for desirable behaviors.
