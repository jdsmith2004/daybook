# Twelve-step and faith-based recovery: outside research for Daybook

Researched 2026-11-01. Outside research only; nothing here evaluates existing Daybook content.

Strength labels: **Strong** (several RCTs or meta-analysis), **Moderate** (a few RCTs or good longitudinal), **Weak** (pilot, cross-sectional, self-report), **Program claim** (expert or program claim only).

How I worked: I fetched primary pages where the sites allowed it. Where a page blocked me (403, ECONNRESET, bot check), or I only got a search-engine summary, I say "secondary" or "not verified". Search summaries were generated by a tool, not read by me in full text. Anything marked "from memory" was not checked.

## PART A: THE TWELVE STEPS

### 1. The steps, and what each asks a person to DO

Text of the SAA steps was read from SAA's own page (https://saa-recovery.org/our-program/the-twelve-steps/). Below is a paraphrase, not a quote (see copyright, below).

| Step | What it says (paraphrase)                                                                      | The concrete DOING behind it                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ---- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Admit powerlessness over the behavior and that life became unmanageable                        | Write or say aloud the honest record of what the habit has cost. SA words it "lust"; SAA "addictive sexual behavior"; SLAA "sex and love addiction" (SLAA step 1 seen at slaauk.org; SA step 1 seen in search snippet only) |
| 2    | Come to believe a Power greater than ourselves could restore sanity                            | A belief step. In practice: stay in the room and keep listening until hope comes                                                                                                                                            |
| 3    | Decide to turn will and life over to God as we understood God                                  | A decision, often a written or spoken prayer with a sponsor. Daily "turning over" afterward                                                                                                                                 |
| 4    | Make a searching and fearless moral inventory                                                  | A written inventory (resentments, fears, harms, sexual conduct, strengths). This is the biggest solitary task                                                                                                               |
| 5    | Admit to God, ourselves and another human being the exact nature of our wrongs                 | Read the inventory aloud to another person (sponsor, clergy). The one step that cannot be done alone                                                                                                                        |
| 6    | Be entirely ready to have God remove the defects                                               | Readiness work with the sponsor: name the patterns you are still protecting                                                                                                                                                 |
| 7    | Humbly ask God to remove the shortcomings                                                      | A prayer, said repeatedly                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| 8    | List all persons harmed, become willing to make amends                                         | A written list, then work on willingness                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| 9    | Make direct amends except when it would injure them or others                                  | Face-to-face or written repair, chosen with the sponsor. Includes the "do no further harm" exception                                                                                                                        |
| 10   | Keep taking personal inventory, promptly admit when wrong                                      | A daily or evening review; quick admission of errors, no waiting                                                                                                                                                            |
| 11   | Seek through prayer and meditation conscious contact with God                                  | A daily practice: quiet time, prayer, meditation                                                                                                                                                                            |
| 12   | Having had a spiritual awakening, carry the message and practice the principles in all affairs | Service: sponsoring, taking meeting commitments, calling newcomers                                                                                                                                                          |

Around the steps sit three non-step actions that make them work in practice: **meetings** (regular, in person or online), a **sponsor** (an experienced member who walks you through the steps), and **a definition of sobriety** each fellowship sets for itself. SA calls its aim "sexual sobriety" and describes it as progressive freedom from lust (SA newcomer material, https://www.sa.org/w/wp-content/uploads/newcomer.pdf, seen in search snippet only). The SLAA site says a sponsor is someone with more time and experience who guides the steps, and a "Recovery Partner" is a more intimate talking partner (https://slaaonline.org/faq/). Wording differences between fellowships are small (the first step's object changes; SAA/SLAA say "God as we understood God"), and the SA page notes SA is stricter about sobriety than SLAA (Wnuk et al. 2023, below, secondary mention).

**Copyright status of the AA wording (read from AA's own pages):**

- AA's site footer: "Copyright (c) 2026 by Alcoholics Anonymous World Services, Inc. All rights reserved" (https://www.aa.org/twelve-steps-alcoholics-anonymous). The steps are published in the book Alcoholics Anonymous, chapter "How It Works".
- AA's content use policy (https://www.aa.org/terms-of-use): local A.A. publications may reprint the Steps without a request; non-A.A. individuals and entities need written approval (requests to ippolicy@aa.org); A.A. material "should not be amended or modified"; and "A.A.W.S. does not issue licenses to reproduce its copyrighted material on mobile apps." It also does not grant permission to continuously reprint excerpts through the internet or electronic devices.
- So an app should not quote the AA steps verbatim and should not claim to be AA's. That is the plain reading of AA's own policy. I am not a lawyer; the owner should get legal advice before relying on this.
- Others adapt them with permission: AAWS board records show a 1984 motion permitting Cocaine Anonymous (Van Nuys) to "reprint for adaptation" the Steps and Traditions (https://museum.ca.org/?p=13728, an AA archivist's note). SA says it received permission from AA to use the steps in 1979 (secondary; SA materials). Al-Anon and Food Addicts Anonymous say AA owns the copyright and written consent was needed (search summaries of al-anon.org and foodaddicts.org pages, secondary).
- SAA protects its own text: "Copyright 1996-2026 ISO of SAA. All Rights Reserved", no copying without written permission (SAA page).
- Practical consequence: ideas and structure (confess to another person, make a list, repair harm, daily review) are not protected. Exact step wording is. A short original paraphrase in the app's own words is the safest way, and I would not name it "the 12 steps". Not legal advice.

### 2. Evidence for 12-step

**Cochrane review (Strong for alcohol only).** Kelly JF, Humphreys K, Ferri M. _Alcoholics Anonymous and other 12-step programs for alcohol use disorder._ Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020, CD012880. Read at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7065341/ .

- 27 studies, 10,565 participants (21 RCT/quasi-RCT, 5 non-randomized, 1 economic-only).
- Manualized AA / 12-step facilitation (TSF) beat other established treatments (MET, CBT) on **continuous abstinence at 12 months: RR 1.21 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.42; 2 studies, 1,936 participants; high certainty)**; the effect held at 24 and 36 months.
- On other outcomes (percent days abstinent, drinking intensity, consequences) manualized TSF was about equal to other treatments; certainties ranged from very low to moderate (e.g. consequences MD -2.88, CI -6.81 to 1.04, moderate; PDA 12 months MD 3.03, CI -4.36 to 10.43, very low).
- Non-manualized AA/TSF looked equal to other treatments, evidence low or very low.
- Limitations in the review: selection bias high-risk in 11 of 27, attrition bias in 9, high heterogeneity for PDA (I-squared 91%).
- Big caveat: "high certainty" rests on only two studies, both of **facilitated** AA (professionals actively linking people to meetings), and all participants had alcohol use disorder. Not porn.

**Project MATCH (Moderate; RCT; alcohol).** Project MATCH Research Group, 1998, _Alcoholism: Clin Exp Res_ 22(6), 3-year outcomes for 952 outpatients. From search summaries (secondary; I did not read the paper): few differences among TSF, CBT and MET; 36% of TSF clients were abstinent at 37 to 39 months versus 24% CBT and 26% MET. Anger was the clearest matching variable (high-anger clients did better with MET).

**What mechanism does the work? (Moderate; secondary analyses of Project MATCH plus reviews).**

- Kelly JF (2017). _Is Alcoholics Anonymous religious, spiritual, neither? Findings from 25 years of mechanisms of behavior change research._ Addiction 112(6):929-936. PMID 27718303. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27718303/ . Conclusion read from PubMed: AA's benefit "seem[s] to be carried predominantly by social, cognitive and affective mechanisms"; the Big Book's spiritual account "may be true only for a minority of participants with high addiction severity."
- Kelly JF, Stout RL, Magill M, Tonigan JS, Pagano ME (2011). _Spirituality in recovery: a lagged mediational analysis of AA's principal theoretical mechanism of behavior change._ Alcohol Clin Exp Res 35(3):454-463. N=1,726 (Project MATCH). Spirituality partially mediated AA's effect: 28% of the AA effect on percent days abstinent in aftercare patients, 14% in outpatients. Attendance raised spiritual practices most among those who started low. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3117904/
- Kelly JF, Hoeppner BB (2012). _Does AA work differently for men and women?_ Drug Alcohol Depend 130:186-193. Six mediators tested: roughly half of AA's effect was explained (abstract reports 53% men and 49% women for percent days abstinent). Social mechanisms (pro-abstinence networks, fewer pro-drinking ties, social self-efficacy) mattered more for men; religiosity was **not** a significant mediator for either sex in this model. Coping with negative affect seemed more relevant for women. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3596430
- Search summary of Kelly-led reviews (secondary): self-efficacy is the most consistent mediator; social networks account for the most variance in days abstinent. I could not retrieve that specific review.
- Kaskutas (quoted in Christian Century, secondary journalism): the strongest predictors of sustained sobriety through AA are having a sponsor, a non-drinking social network, and commitment to service. Treat as an expert statement, not a checked finding.

Honest reading: social ties and self-efficacy carry most of the effect; spirituality carries a real but partial share, mostly in the more severely affected. **Nothing here shows you must complete all twelve steps.** The evidence is about attendance, active involvement and networks, not step completion.

**Atheists and agnostics.** Tonigan JS, Miller WR, Schermer C (2002). _Atheists, agnostics and Alcoholics Anonymous._ J Stud Alcohol 63(5):534-541. PMID 12380849. Project MATCH, N=1,526. Non-believers attended AA less, but **attendance was linked to more abstinence and less drinking regardless of God belief**; clients "unsure" about God had higher drinking frequency. Moderate (secondary analysis of RCT data).

**Evidence for sex-related 12-step groups: thin.**

- A 2024 systematic review exists: _Using Twelve-Step Treatment for Sex Addiction and Compulsive Sexual Behaviour (Disorder): A Systematic Review of the Literature._ Sexual Health & Compulsivity 31(2):170-188 (Andersson, Carlstrom, Amroussia, Lindroth). I could not open the full text (403). A project page at Malmo University summarizes the research as "very limited" (secondary). https://www.informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/26929953.2024.2339208
- Wnuk M, Krentzman AR, Charzynska E (2023). _The 12-Step Pathway to Spiritual Growth and Gratitude and Its Relationship With Well-Being Among the Members of Sexaholics Anonymous in Poland._ J Relig Health 63(1):370-392. N=80 (72 men), cross-sectional snowball survey, over 85% religious, mostly Roman Catholic. More steps completed was linked to lower negative affect (beta = -0.32) and more spiritual growth (beta = 0.24), and indirectly to gratitude and life satisfaction through spiritual growth. Single-item measure of steps done. **Weak.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10861752/
- Wnuk & Charzynska (2022), J Behav Addict: 80 Polish SA members, involvement linked to life satisfaction via meaning in life and hope (search summary, secondary). Weak.
- A paper on SA step progress and compulsive sexual behavior (PMC6174596) reports that being further along the steps was related to lower CSB and sexual suppression; it called for longitudinal replication. I could not open it (bot check); known only from a search summary. Weak.
- Fernandez, Kuss, Griffiths (2021): qualitative, 14 SLAA members (search summary, secondary). Weak.
- A 2023 scoping review of 47 studies of non-AA 12-step groups found compulsive sexual behavior groups were 11% of the studies (search summary of Eur Arch Psychiatry Clin Neurosci, DOI 10.1007/s00406-023-01667-x; paywalled). Weak.
- Bottom line: for porn and sex specifically there is **no RCT of 12-step groups** that I could find. All support is cross-sectional or small. Anything claiming "12-step works for porn" borrows from the alcohol literature.

### 3. Why people value the steps, and what an app can and cannot carry

Reasons people give or that follow from the design (my synthesis; the Glassman et al. qualitative review below supports the identity and "rock-bottom" themes):

1. **The sequence.** A path in order, so you are never asking "what do I do now?"
2. **A finish line.** There is a "done with the steps" moment, though the program says it never ends.
3. **Doing rather than reading.** Steps 4, 5, 8, 9 are tasks that cost something. You cannot do them passively.
4. **Identity.** "I am a recovering addict" gives a name for the person you are now.
5. **Fellowship.** Someone knows. The sponsor and the room are the engine.

| Quality             | Can an app carry it alone?                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sequence            | Yes. Easy.                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| A finish            | Yes, as a marker the person earns, not a gate.                                                                                                                                                            |
| Doing, not reading  | Yes for the solo tasks (inventory, daily review, quiet time, a letter written). No for tasks that need another person.                                                                                    |
| Identity            | Partly. The app can reflect back who you are becoming. Only people confirm it.                                                                                                                            |
| Fellowship, sponsor | **No.** A solo app cannot be this, and a fake version (an AI "sponsor" or a bot "accountability partner") is the biggest bolted-on risk. It can only point to, prepare for, and nudge toward real people. |

**Critiques (all Weak; mostly small qualitative studies).**

- Glassman, Rhodes, Buus, critical review of 21 qualitative interview studies, 1977 to 2014: themes of "rock-bottom" and powerlessness, and identity change. (Search summary, secondary: https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/a-critical-review-of-qualitative-interview-studies-with-alcoholic/ ). The same group's interview study of six Sydney members found the AA "master narrative" includes powerlessness, internalized pathology, and AA as the only solution (secondary). Women's thesis samples (Fleming 2006, six women) criticize powerlessness as fostering disempowerment, while another small study (Myllmaki, undergrad review) found women generally found Step 1 helpful. Treat as a split, not a verdict.
- Rankine (UWE thesis, four UK men): shame and the recovering-alcoholic identity (secondary).
- Caldwell & Cutter (1998), J Subst Abuse Treat, 55 patients: dropout is high in the first three months after treatment, and less enthusiasm for the higher-power concept went with more relapse (search summary, secondary).
- For people with religious shame, see Part B: for them a punishing frame can add harm.

### 4. Secular paths with a clear sequence

- **SMART Recovery 4-Point Program** (https://smartrecoveryaustralia.com.au/en/what-are-the-four-points-of-the-smart-recovery-program ; search summaries of national sites): (1) Build and maintain motivation, (2) Cope with urges, (3) Manage thoughts, feelings and behaviors, (4) Live a balanced life. The points are a toolbox, not a strict order: sources say one does not go straight from 1 to 4. It is used for sexual addiction as well as substances. Evidence: Beck AK, Forbes E, Baker AL, Kelly PJ, Deane FP, Shakeshaft A, Hunt D, Kelly JF (2017). _Systematic review of SMART Recovery: outcomes, process variables, and implications for research._ Psychol Addict Behav 31(1):1-20. PMID 28165272. 12 studies, only 3 evaluating effectiveness; authors say positive effects but cannot make conclusive remarks about efficacy. **Weak to Moderate.**
- **Refuge Recovery** (Noah Levine): Four Truths of recovery and the Eightfold Path, non-theistic, meditation and community (search summary of publisher and library records, secondary). No outcome data found.
- **Recovery Dharma** (https://recoverydharma.org/, read): renunciation, meditation, meetings, the Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path in daily life, written inquiry, wise friends and mentors, generosity and service; autonomous local groups. No outcome data found.
- **LifeRing** (https://www.lifering.org/, read): "3-S": Sobriety, Secularity, Self-Empowerment. One commitment (no drinking or using no matter what), meetings built on cross-talk, a personal recovery plan. Site cites no outcome data (links an efficacy report I did not open).
- Pattern across them: every one has some version of **honest account, daily practice, other people, repair/service**. The secular ones replace the Higher Power with a practice (meditation) or a plan.
- Spiritual Self-Schema (3-S+) therapy (Margolin, Avants; Yale): manualized therapy that works inside a client's own beliefs; preliminary studies (n=15 and n=38) showed longer abstinence and lower impulsivity; a 120-person RCT (NCT01305629) is registered but I found no published results. **Weak.** (search summary; registry https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01305629)

## PART B: FAITH-BASED RECOVERY

### 5. How these programs work

I could verify few of them in detail. Where I could not, I say so.

- **Celebrate Recovery (CR).** Read at https://markcole.ca/celebrate-recovery (a secondary church-author page) and Wikipedia. Eight principles, built on the Beatitudes (Matthew 5), spell R-E-C-O-V-E-R-Y and map onto the 12 steps in order: (1) realize I am not God and life is unmanageable alone, (2) believe God exists and can help, (3) choose to entrust life and will to Christ, (4) honestly examine and confess faults to myself, God and a trusted person, (5) accept God's changes, (6) evaluate relationships, forgive, make amends, (7) daily time for self-examination, Bible and prayer, (8) yield to God to share the good news. Format: large-group worship and teaching, same-gender small groups, accountability partners, a multi-month Step Study (the page says over 5 million have completed one), run by trained church leaders using only the Bible and approved curriculum (Wikipedia). **Outcome data:** Kelly and White (2012) state there is "no empirical evidence regarding the impacts or efficacy"; a 2011 J Relig Health study (Brown et al.) found higher spirituality went with more confidence resisting substance use (both via Wikipedia, secondary). Program claim only.
- **Pure Desire (Ted Roberts).** The home page (https://puredesire.org/) describes Breakthrough Groups led by people with lived experience, a free call, 30 days of daily support, and counseling for betrayal trauma. Per listings, the _7 Pillars of Freedom_ is a weekly group study of about 10 months with workbook and journal (booksellers, secondary). **I could not find or verify the names of the seven pillars.** Outcome claims on the site (150K+ served since 2016, 1,000+ "found freedom" in 2025) have no stated measure. Program claim only.
- **Fortify (Fight the New Drug).** Run by a non-religious nonprofit (search summaries: Fight the New Drug describes itself as non-religious and science-only). Video-based program, lifetime adult access reported at $39, free for teens, estimated 2 to 5 months to complete (secondary). I could not load fortifyprogram.org (ECONNRESET). So it is a **secular** example, not a faith program. No outcome data found.
- **Covenant Eyes / Victory app.** Read at https://www.covenanteyes.com/victory/ : daily check-in with a feeling picker and trigger notes, an invited "ally" who gets notifications and reports of device activity, expert-guided lessons, a community feed with prayer requests; endorsed by AACC. Claims 1.7 million users. Marketing only. Program claim only.
- **Every Man's Battle (Arterburn, Stoeker, 2000; 20th ed. 2020).** A book and guide with a Christian approach to sexual temptation. I could not verify the program structure from search; the description of "seven weapons" or "five principles" I had in mind is **not confirmed**. Program claim only.
- **Exodus 90 (Catholic).** Read at https://exodus90.com/ : 90 days of formation (daily reflection), asceticism (exercise three times a week; fast and no meat Wednesday and Friday; limit non-essential entertainment), prayer (up to an hour daily, at least 20 minutes), and fraternity (a weekly group of 4 to 6 men plus a daily check-in with a paired "anchor"). Self-reported "over 99%" feel greater freedom, 300,000+ men; no independent verification. Program claim only.
- **Integrity Restored (Catholic).** Site (https://integrityrestored.com/) lists coaching, a betrayal trauma program, a self-test, a litany; the home page did not describe format, steps or outcomes. Not verified further.
- **Guard Your Eyes (Jewish).** Read at https://guardyoureyes.com/ : a "Three Pillar Program": Motivation, Planning, Connection (one-on-one support, forum, finding an "ally"); content "rooted in behavioral science, psychology, and Torah"; free and anonymous. The page claims "Guaranteed" and "over 90000 champions" with no outcome data. A related Torah-based program (JewishSexuality.com, via search) says it adds Torah study and mikvah to AA-style guidelines. An academic paper (via search) argues GYE presents porn as disease. Program claim only.
- **Purify Your Gaze (Muslim).** Founder Zeyad Ramadan; a 12-week pilot course in 2010 with 12 people; anonymous; several thousand members self-reported (aboutislam.net, secondary). I could not load purifyyourgaze.com. Program claim only.

**Takeaway across all of them:** every faith program contains the same four human moves: (a) tell the truth to someone, (b) a daily rhythm of prayer or scripture or equivalent, (c) a small group or partner, (d) some cost or discipline (fasting, filters, time). None has independent outcome data for porn that I could find.

### 6. Evidence on spirituality and recovery

- **Positive vs negative religious coping.** Ano GG, Vasconcelles EB (2005). _Religious coping and psychological adjustment to stress: a meta-analysis._ J Clin Psychol 61(4). PMID 15503316. 49 studies, 105 effect sizes. Positive religious coping (e.g. benevolent reappraisal, seeking support) went with better adjustment; negative religious coping (e.g. feeling punished or abandoned by God) went with worse adjustment. (Pargament's RCOPE: Pargament, Koenig, Perez 2000, J Clin Psychol 56, per search.) **Moderate (meta-analysis, but correlational).**
- **Religious and spiritual struggles predict later distress.** Bockrath MF, Pargament KI, Wong S, Harriott VA, Pomerleau JM, Homolka SJ, Chaudhary ZB, Exline JJ (2021, issue 2022). _Religious and spiritual struggles and their links to psychological adjustment: a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies._ Psychol Relig Spiritual. DOI 10.1037/rel0000400. Across 32 studies, struggles predicted later negative adjustment, a small effect (Zr = 0.08, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.10); no clear effect on positive adjustment (12 studies). Figures from a search summary (secondary). **Moderate, small effect.**
- **Divine struggle (anger at God, feeling punished or abandoned).** Exline's work: disclosure of anger at God helps when met with an empathetic response; only about 2.5% reported frequent intense anger in a national sample (search summary, secondary). **Weak to Moderate.**
- **Spiritually integrated psychotherapy.** Captari LE, Hook JN, Hoyt W, Davis DE, McElroy-Heltzel SE, Worthington EL (2018). _Integrating clients' religion and spirituality within psychotherapy: a comprehensive meta-analysis._ J Clin Psychol 74(11):1938-1951. PMID 30221353. 97 outcome studies, N=7,181. Religion/spirituality-adapted therapy beat no-treatment and non-R/S therapy (g = 0.74 psychological, g = 0.74 spiritual, vs controls). In the more rigorous "additive" designs (same therapy, with or without spiritual accommodation), it was **equally effective for distress (g = 0.13, p = 0.258)** and better for spiritual well-being (g = 0.34). Read from PubMed. **Moderate.** Meaning: adapting to belief helps spiritual outcomes and does no harm to the rest; it does not clearly make the therapy work better for distress.
- **Moral incongruence and porn (most relevant).**
  - Grubbs JB, Exline JJ, Pargament KI, Hook JN, Carlisle RD (2015) and follow-ups: religiosity predicts perceived porn addiction, mediated by moral disapproval, and this persists when actual use is controlled (search summary). Moderate (cross-sectional plus longitudinal follow-ups).
  - Grubbs JB, Perry SL, Wilt JA, Reid RC (2019; author list from memory, title/journal/PMID read from PubMed). _Pornography problems due to moral incongruence: an integrative model with a systematic review and meta-analysis._ Arch Sex Behav 48(2):397-415. PMID 30076491. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30076491/ . A secondary review quotes aggregate r = 0.31 for religiosity with self-perceived problems, with a stronger path through moral incongruence; I could not verify these numbers in the paper itself. **Moderate.**
  - Grubbs JB, Exline JJ, Pargament KI, Volk F, Lindberg MJ (2017). _Internet pornography use, perceived addiction, and religious/spiritual struggles._ Arch Sex Behav 46(6):1733-1745. PMID 27351579. Two studies (N=1,519 and N=713); perceived addiction, not actual use, predicted later religious/spiritual struggle over a year (n=156 and n=366 follow-ups). Read from PubMed. **Moderate.**
  - Perry SL & Whitehead AL (2019), J Sex Res 56(1):50-61: stronger religious ties went with lower sexual satisfaction as porn use rose (search summary). Perry (2018) Society and Mental Health reports porn use predicted depression over time only among men who disapproved (blog summary; verify before use). **Weak (secondary).**
  - In plain terms: for religious people, the sense of being addicted tracks moral disapproval more than hours of use, and feeling "addicted" predicts later spiritual struggle. A frame of sin, impurity and God's disappointment can raise distress. This does not mean the habit is harmless; it means the _story_ a person tells about it changes how they hurt.
- **Shame and recovery.** Small studies link shame-proneness to relapse and self-compassion to less shame (n of 10 to 62, several pre/post, some dissertations; secondary). **Weak.** I did not find a good trial separating guilt from shame in addiction, and I did not research mindfulness or relapse-prevention trials here.
- **Grace vs law framing.** I found **no controlled study** comparing grace-framed to law-framed recovery content. Inference from the coping and struggle research above: content that pictures God as punishing or distant matches "negative religious coping" and is associated with worse adjustment. This is reasoned from correlational evidence, not tested head to head. Say so honestly in anything built on it.
- **What makes faith content help vs hurt (my synthesis, labeled as such):** helps when it offers a loving, present God or a calm practice, a person to confess to who responds with acceptance, community, meaning, and a way back after failure. Hurts when it frames the person as impure or watched, makes spiritual standing depend on performance (streaks as holiness), treats a slip as a spiritual failure, or when the person is already in moral conflict and the program adds more of it.

### 7. How faith apps let belief be a choice (limited verification)

- **Hallow, Glorify, YouVersion, Pray.com, Abide** are faith-first apps. They presume belief and personalize by interest and goal, not by whether you believe. Hallow's onboarding (third-party UX showcases only, screensdesign.com) is a name screen, an interest grid (prayer, meditation), a goal grid and a notifications prompt, with a paywall after a quiz. Abide publishes a statement of faith (Nicene Creed). YouVersion is free with no ads (its own page). **I could not verify any of these asking whether the user believes, and I could not verify what they change after an answer.** My reading: they do not treat belief as a choice, because their whole audience is already believing. They are not good models for a mixed audience.
- **Recovery apps with a faith option.** I found no verified example of a shared app that asks and branches. What exists, per app-store listings (developer claims, not verified): separate faith-only apps (Hope: Christian Recovery, Faitheal, Redemption: Prayer Recovery, OnlyFaith, Twenty-Four Hours a Day), and secular apps (Fortify is secular by its parent's mission). Reframe and I Am Sober: I could not confirm a faith option from search. So the usual pattern is **two separate products, not one product with a choice.** A single app that makes belief optional and does not make the non-believer feel like a guest would be unusual.
- **Does "Higher Power" language work for non-believers, and do Christians accept it?**
  - Tonigan 2002 (above): God belief unimportant for benefit, but non-believers attend less, so the language is a barrier to starting even if not to benefiting. Moderate.
  - AA's own pamphlet says AA "is not a religious organization" and has room for all shades of belief and non-belief (via search snippet, secondary). A blog count says the Big Book uses the exact phrase "Higher Power" rarely and "a Power greater than ourselves" more often (blog, secondary).
  - Secular AA members report friction and the need to find a sponsor who will not push (AA Agnostica qualitative analysis; self-selected readers, Weak).
  - A reporter's piece on Zachary Munro's dissertation (Catholic Register) says many in secular groups heard "higher power" as a step toward God, and religious members sometimes feel the vague term dilutes their faith (secondary). I found no study of how Christians feel about a generic Higher Power. Treat that as an open question.
  - Design reading: a generic "something bigger" is accepted by many but neither side is fully served. A choice of words at the point of use beats a single compromise word.

## OPINION: DOING MOVES THAT MAKE RECOVERY REAL

The shared set across 12-step and the faith and secular programs, ordered as a person would meet them. For each, what a solo app can do.

| #   | Move                                                                                                        | Solo app?                              | What it would take                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Tell the truth about the pattern** to yourself, in writing (Step 1, SMART point 1, GYE motivation)        | **Yes, mostly**                        | One honest written account: what it costs, what it gives, when it happens. Plain words, no "powerless" or "addict" label required. The log and pattern work already do half of this                                                                                               |
| 2   | **Name what steadies you** (Steps 2 and 3: a Power, God, a purpose, a person)                               | **Yes, as a choice**                   | One early question with a real "no, none of the above" answer. Store the answer and use it for wording only (see below)                                                                                                                                                           |

### The real insight of "you have to do all twelve"

It is not "twelve is a magic number" and the evidence does not say you must finish them. The insight is that **recovery is made of things you do, in an order, with other people, that cost something**, and that reading about recovery is not recovery. A program with a sequence and a finish gives the person a daily answer to "what do I do now?", moves the identity from "someone fighting" to "someone who did these things," and refuses to let insight stand in for action. Also, step 5 is a real forcing function: the path asks you to tell another human, and most solo programs never ask.

### How an app can honor it without pretending to be a fellowship

1. **Make a short path of deeds, not lessons.** Each stage ends in something done outside the app (wrote it, said it to someone, set the plan, made the call). The lesson is the preparation; the deed is the step.
2. **Put one real human into the path and say so.** Tell one person. It is fine, even good, to say plainly: "This part cannot be done in an app."
3. **Keep a visible order and a visible finish** that are honest: each stage is one deed; finishing is celebrated; then the daily practice continues. No lock-ups: care tools never wait on the path.
4. **Do not copy AA's wording.** Use plain original words for the same actions.
5. **Do not demand the identity.** The 12-step self-label ("I am an addict") is the most-criticized piece; the evidence for naming a lifelong label is weak. A person can do every action without taking the label.
6. **Ask for the human step at moments of strength, not after a slip.**

### How to let belief be a choice without it getting complicated

- **Ask once, early, softly,** with answers worded as the person's own: something like "Do you want prayer or faith in this?" with options: "Yes, my faith matters to this", "Open to it / spiritual but not religious", "No, keep it practical." A fourth quiet "ask me later" is fine. It is not a quiz and not a gate. It must be easy to change later in settings, and it must not reappear as a nag.
- **Change only three things** after the answer: (a) the closing line of a lesson or practice (a prayer or a plain reflection), (b) the wording at steps 2, 3 and 11 ("God", "something bigger than me", "what steadies me"), and (c) which "quiet minute" choices appear first. Everything else, including every care tool, stays identical. This keeps the code and the app simple.
- **For believers, let them pick a tradition's language** (Christian, Catholic, Jewish, Muslim, general) only if the owner can write it well. If not, one generic "faith" choice with the person's own words (a blank "my prayer" field) beats badly written text in five traditions.
- **For everyone, grace first.** Evidence points that way (negative coping and spiritual struggle track worse adjustment; Part B) though no one has tested grace vs law directly. Never present a slip as a spiritual failure. For a person in moral conflict, present both "this is struggling with something you wish you did differently" and room to talk to someone about it, without telling them that they are addicted or that they are not.
- **Believers who find the app too thin** should be pointed to CR, a church group, or a tradition-specific program. The app should not try to be a church.

### What would be cheap or bolted on (avoid)

- A "verse of the day" or a prayer-of-the-day card not tied to what the person just did.
- Swapping "Higher Power" for "God" or the reverse throughout, so one side is always a guest.
- Faith-gated or step-gated content (locking lessons behind completing a step, or locking care behind belief).
- Streaks or points for prayer. A prayer streak turns prayer into a score and reintroduces shame at the first missed day.
- Calling a step by a spiritual name when it is only a worksheet ("Step 4") with no real action behind it.
- An AI "sponsor", AI "accountability partner" or fake community member. It imitates the one piece an app cannot carry and could do harm.
- A sin or impurity list as the inventory.
- Showing the faith question as the first screen, or in a way that makes "no" feel like the wrong answer.
- Reading-only "steps" that are just text, or checking a box without a real-world act.
- Using AA's numbered step list, the phrase "the Twelve Steps", or other AA marks as a selling point.
- Branching into five separate faith experiences with thin, copy-paste text.
- Promising "freedom" or "guaranteed" results (Guard Your Eyes, Exodus 90, Victory all do; none show outcome data).
