# What the research supports for a "how to quit" curriculum (Daybook)

Researched 1 November 2026. Purpose: decide what the daily lessons should teach a person who wants to quit porn (or another habit) without shame, and how strongly each piece is backed.

## How to read this

**Strength labels** (as requested): Strong = several RCTs or a meta-analysis. Moderate = a few RCTs or good longitudinal work. Weak = pilot, cross-sectional, self-report, lab-only, or single small trial. Expert view only = theory, review opinion, or clinical lore.

**Verification tags** (how I got each fact):

- **[opened]** I opened the abstract or article page and read it.
- **[secondary]** I only saw it in a search-engine summary or a news, press-release or blog page. I did not open the source. Treat the numbers as leads to confirm before quoting them in the app.
- Nothing here comes from memory. Where I could not check a claim, I say so.

**Limits of this research.** The search tool hit its quota partway through. Items I did not get to are listed in "Gaps" at the end. Several publisher pages (Springer, Taylor & Francis, some PubMed abstracts) were blocked, so some effect sizes are missing.

## The short version (ten findings that should shape the curriculum)

1. **Problematic pornography use (PPU) has a thin treatment evidence base.** A 2022 preregistered review found 24 studies, only 4 of them RCTs, mostly men, mostly waitlist controls, most scoring low on quality. The best-supported approach is CBT-style skills, with ACT next. Strength: Weak-to-Moderate. [opened]
2. **Skills-based programs that look like Daybook's plan (triggers, habits, cravings, negative thoughts, relapse prevention) have the best PPU data**, but the one web trial lost 89% of its intervention group. Strength: Weak. [opened]
3. **ACT worked in a small RCT with religious men** (28 men, nearly all Latter-day Saints): viewing fell 93% vs 21% on waitlist. This is the nearest thing to evidence that a faith-adjacent audience can be helped without arguing about their beliefs. Strength: Weak-to-Moderate (one small RCT). [opened]
4. **If-then plans work, but modestly, and less for stopping things than starting them.** Overall d = .65 (94 tests); for alcohol and tobacco reductions about g = .31 in RCTs, and for weekly drinking only d+ = -0.14 in a stricter review in which every study was at high risk of bias. One plan beats three. Strength: Moderate for health behaviour in general, Weak for quitting, none for porn. [opened]
5. **Shame is a bad bet but "shame causes relapse" is less proven than people say.** The best prospective lapse study (203 smokers) found first-lapse guilt and self-blame did NOT predict relapse; low confidence after the lapse and a bigger lapse did. Teach "keep the lapse small and keep your confidence," not "guilt will make you relapse." Strength: Moderate (smoking only). [opened]
6. **Moral incongruence is the strongest known predictor of feeling addicted to porn, bigger than how much a person watches.** Religiosity predicts it. A faith-leaning app can make this worse if it adds moral condemnation. Strength: Moderate (many cross-sectional samples plus one year-long longitudinal sample). [opened/secondary]
7. **Identity "I am someone who..." has appeal but weak proof.** The famous "be a voter" noun-vs-verb effect failed to replicate in several field experiments. Identity-based motivation and social identity in recovery have qualitative and theory support. Strength: Weak. [opened/secondary]
8. **Popular "reboot" claims are mostly unsupported.** A 7-day abstinence RCT (n=176) found nothing on its confirmed outcomes; forum reports show more than half of posters relapsed; motives for abstaining track beliefs (masturbation is unhealthy, religiosity) more than symptom severity. The "90 days rewires the brain" claim has no controlled source I could find. [opened/secondary]
9. **Streaks have a measurable downside.** Broken streaks reduce later engagement, mostly when the person blames themselves (7 experiments, not addiction). Strength: Moderate for the mechanism, none in addiction. [secondary]
10. **Nobody has good long-term data on what predicts lasting recovery from PPU.** What exists is cross-sectional (stress and emotional avoidance as motives, loneliness, shame, moral incongruence). Say so; do not invent a "science of staying quit." Strength: Weak.

# 1. Treatments

## 1A. Problematic pornography use / compulsive sexual behavior disorder (CSBD)

### Overall state of the evidence

- **Claim:** Treatment appears to help PPU/CSBD symptoms, with the most support for CBT, but strong conclusions about which treatment works best are not justified.
- **Source:** Antons et al. 2022, _Journal of Behavioral Addictions_ 11(3), 643-666. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9872540 [opened]
- **Design:** Preregistered systematic review, 24 studies (2000-2021), 4 RCTs.
- **Limits the authors list:** mostly waitlist controls; about 95% male and 84% heterosexual; small samples; 14 different symptom scales; most studies low or very low quality, only one preregistered; they warn that "the effect of taking part in a clinical trial may be higher than the specific effect of the treatment itself."
- **Strength:** Weak-to-Moderate.
- **Design implication:** Do not tell users "studies prove this works." Say "this approach has helped people in early studies."

### CBT, group (Hallberg)

- **Claim:** Seven weeks of group CBT reduced hypersexual symptoms and sexual compulsivity more than waitlist, and the gains held at 3 and 6 months.
- **Source:** Hallberg, Kaldo, Arver et al. 2019, _Journal of Sexual Medicine_ 16(5), 733-745. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30956109/ [opened]
- **Design/sample:** RCT, 137 men diagnosed with hypersexual disorder (70 CBT, 67 waitlist; Antons lists 58 and 54, presumably those analysed). Waitlist got the treatment after 8 weeks. Primary outcome HD:CAS.
- **Caveats:** I could not get effect sizes. Follow-up response was low, so long-term effects are uncertain. Men only. Components were not dismantled, so we do not know which part of CBT worked.
- **A 2020 internet version** from the same group was a 36-man single-arm pilot (no control group): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32900671/ [secondary]
- **Strength:** Moderate for CBT in clinically diagnosed men (one decent RCT), Weak for a self-guided app.

### CBT/MI/mindfulness web self-help ("Hands-off", Bőthe)

- **Claim:** A free six-week web program for PPU lowered problem scores and use more than a waitlist among people who finished, but most people did not finish.
- **Source:** Bőthe et al. 2021, _Journal of Behavioral Addictions_ 10(4), 1015-1035. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8987418 [opened]
- **Design/sample:** Two-arm RCT, 264 adults (123 program, 141 waitlist), 96% men, mostly US/England/Canada.
- **Content:** six modules: motivation to change, spotting risky situations, building habits and enjoyable activities, managing cravings, challenging negative thoughts, preventing relapse. Daily diary and progress graph. Methods drew on motivational interviewing, CBT, mindfulness and social-psychological ("wise") interventions.
- **Results (completers, 6 weeks):** PPU score d = 1.32; use frequency d = 1.65; self-perceived addiction d = 0.85; avoidance self-efficacy d = 0.87; craving d = 0.40. Moral incongruence did not change.
- **Caveats:** only 34.5% completed follow-up (11% of the program group vs 55% of the waitlist), mean 1.73 modules completed, intention-to-treat analysis was not possible, only 13 program participants in the final model. The authors call it a first step.
- **Strength:** Weak. It is the closest published structure to what Daybook plans, and its attrition (about 12% finished module 6) is a warning about long programs.

### ACT (Twohig and Crosby)

- **Claim:** Acceptance and commitment therapy sharply cut porn viewing in men who wanted to stop, mostly religious men, without changing their beliefs.
- **Sources:**
  - Twohig & Crosby 2010 pilot, _Behavior Therapy_ 41, 285-295. https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/psych_facpub/1700 [opened]: 6 men, multiple-baseline, eight 1.5-hour sessions, viewing down about 85% at post and 83% at 3 months, quality of life improved, psychological flexibility improved a lot, scrupulosity scores fell.
  - Crosby & Twohig 2016, _Behavior Therapy_ 47(3), 355-366. https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/psych_facpub/1355 [opened]: RCT, 28 adult men, all but one Latter-day Saint, 12 individual sessions vs waitlist. Viewing down 93% (ACT) vs 21% (waitlist). Across everyone treated: 92% reduction post, 86% at 3 months; complete cessation 54% post and 35% at 3 months; at least 70% reduction in 93% post and 74% at follow-up.
  - Levin et al. 2017, _The Family Journal_ 25, 306-312: ACT self-help book, 19 adults, open trial, problems and cognitive fusion improved; only 45% of post-assessors read at least half the book. [secondary, plus Antons table which lists it, opened]
- **Caveats:** The 2016 RCT's quality-of-life measure did not change (Antons table, opened). Waitlist control. "Reduction" is not abstinence; many people were still viewing. The sample was nearly all one denomination.
- **Strength:** Weak-to-Moderate. One small RCT plus a pilot and an open trial.
- **Why it matters for Daybook:** The treatment does not tell people their moral view is wrong or right. It builds willingness to feel urges and discomfort while acting on stated values.

### Mindfulness for PPU

- Holas et al. 2020 (n=13) lowered porn use and depression without changing symptom severity; Sniewski et al. 2020 (n=12, single-case) improved on PPCS in 7 of 11. Both are tiny and uncontrolled. Strength: Weak. [opened, via Antons table]

### What I did not find for PPU

- No RCT of urge surfing, implementation intentions, self-compassion, accountability software, 12-step groups, or "identity" work for porn specifically. The evidence for all of these is borrowed from other habits.

## 1B. Habits and addictions in general

### Relapse prevention (Marlatt and Gordon)

- **Claim:** Relapse prevention, the skills package of identifying high-risk situations and rehearsing coping responses, modestly reduces substance use and improves day-to-day functioning.
- **Source:** Irvin, Bowers, Dunn & Wang 1999, _J Consulting & Clinical Psychology_ 67(4), 563-570 (meta-analysis, 26 studies, 9,504 people; r = .14 for substance use, r = .48 for psychosocial functioning; stronger for alcohol and mixed-substance, weaker for tobacco and cocaine), as summarised in Hendershot, Witkiewitz, George & Marlatt 2011, _Substance Abuse Treatment, Prevention, and Policy_ 6:17. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3163190 [opened - the Irvin numbers are second-hand from this review]
- **Also there:** skills-based RP for smoking gained support only in some re-analyses (self-help OR 1.52; group counselling OR 2.55). RP has been absorbed into most CBT programs, so it is hard to test alone.
- **Strength:** Strong for "modest effect" (meta-analysis) but old and small in size for use.

### The reformulation (Witkiewitz and Marlatt)

- **Claim:** Relapse is a dynamic process, not a single switch: slow-building background risks (stress, mood, social pressure) interact with sudden triggers, and the person's response in that moment is the critical point.
- **Source:** Witkiewitz & Marlatt 2004, _American Psychologist_ 59, 224-235, described in Hendershot 2011 (opened above) and secondary sources. I did not read the paper itself. [secondary]
- **Strength:** Expert view only (a theory). Useful for lesson design because it justifies teaching both "tonic" risks (sleep, stress, loneliness) and "phasic" triggers (cue plus urge).

### Abstinence violation effect (AVE)

- **Claim:** The idea that a lapse is read as "I failed, so I might as well continue" is plausible, but direct evidence is thinner and more mixed than the textbook version.
- **Sources:**
  - Hendershot 2011 (above, opened): direct empirical support for AVE is limited; one EMA study of social drinkers found limit violations predicted AVE-like responses the next day, and distress about violations predicted more drinking.
  - Kirchner, Shiffman & Wileyto 2012, _J Abnormal Psychology_ 121(1), 187-197. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289 [opened]. 203 smokers, 1,001 lapses, electronic diaries, 6 weeks. Responses to the FIRST lapse (self-blame, guilt, self-efficacy) did not predict relapse. Lower self-efficacy after the first lapse did predict faster return to daily smoking (HR 1.11). Across later lapses each 1-point drop in self-efficacy raised the hazard of the next lapse by 9%. Self-blame was slightly protective (HR 0.98), which contradicts the theory. Smoking more during a lapse predicted a faster next lapse (HR 1.17). Each lapse followed the previous one about 8% sooner. Limits: motivated, mostly White, nicotine-patch trial, 6 weeks.
- **Strength:** Moderate for smoking (one good prospective study); Weak for porn.
- **What to teach:** Lapses can cluster and get closer together, so the first hour and day after a slip matter. Confidence ("I can still do this") and size of the lapse matter more than how bad the person feels. Do not assert in the app that guilt makes people relapse; the best study does not show that.

### Mindfulness-based relapse prevention (MBRP) and urge surfing

- **Claim:** Mindfulness training in relapse prevention is promising, but pooled evidence does not show it beats other treatments at preventing relapse.
- **Sources:**
  - Bowen et al. 2014, _JAMA Psychiatry_: RCT, 286 adults after treatment for substance use disorder; both MBRP and standard RP cut relapse risk to drug use and heavy drinking vs treatment as usual; at one year MBRP showed less heavy drinking and fewer drug-use days than standard RP. Press figures (about 9% vs 14% vs 17% using drugs at one year) are from a news item and unchecked. PDF: https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Article-1_Bowen-et-al-JAMA-2014.pdf [secondary]
  - Grant et al. 2017, _J Addiction Medicine_ 11(5), 386-396. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5636047 [opened]. 9 RCTs, 901 people. Relapse OR 0.72 (95% CI 0.46-1.13), not significant. Small effects on craving (SMD -0.13) and negative consequences (SMD -0.23), "limited confidence."
  - Goldberg et al. 2021, Cochrane Review of mindfulness-based interventions for substance use disorders. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8527365/ [opened]. 40 RCTs, 2,825 people in analyses. Versus no treatment: certainty very low. Versus other treatments: may slightly reduce percentage of days of use (low certainty). Almost no harms reported.
  - Brewer et al. 2011, _Drug and Alcohol Dependence_ 119, 72-80: 88 smokers, mindfulness training vs American Lung Association program, abstinence 31% vs 6% at 17 weeks (36% vs 15% at end of treatment, not significant). [secondary]
- **Strength:** Moderate at best, honestly Weak-to-Moderate. "Promising, safe, not proven better than usual care."

**Urge surfing specifically.**

- Bowen & Marlatt 2009, _Psychology of Addictive Behaviors_ 23(4), 666-671. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20025372/ [secondary]. 123 undergraduate smokers randomised to brief "surf the urge" instructions or none: urges did NOT differ, but the surfing group smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over 7 days. Mechanism: changes the response to the urge, not the urge itself.
- Elwafi et al. 2013, _Drug and Alcohol Dependence_ 130, 222-229 [secondary]: 33 adults in a mindfulness arm; the link between craving and smoking weakened from r = .58 to r = .13 by end of treatment in people who practiced informally. No control group.
- Witkiewitz et al. 2013, _Addictive Behaviors_ 38(2), 1563-1571 [secondary]: acceptance and awareness mediated MBRP's effect on craving after treatment.
- **Strength for urge surfing:** Weak (one brief RCT in college smokers, one uncontrolled mediation study). The idea "the urge passes and you do not have to act on it" is consistent across these studies and across ACT.

### ACT for shame in substance use

- **Claim:** A six-hour ACT module aimed at shame, added to residential treatment, reduced shame more by the 4-month follow-up and led to better treatment attendance and fewer days of use.
- **Source:** Luoma, Kohlenberg, Hayes & Fletcher 2012, _J Consulting & Clinical Psychology_ 80, 43-51. https://contextualscience.org/publications/slow_and_steady_wins_the_race_a_randomized_clinica [secondary]
- **Design:** 133 patients in a 28-day residential program, cohort pairs randomised to treatment as usual or ACT-for-shame. Shame fell slower right after treatment but more by 4 months; effects on later use were mediated by treatment attendance.
- **Strength:** Weak-to-Moderate (one RCT, substance use not porn).

### Self-compassion after a lapse

- **Claim:** Treating a failure with kindness, rather than harshness, does not reduce motivation to improve and may raise it.
- **Source:** Breines & Chen 2012, _Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin_ 38(9), 1133-1143. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22645164/ [secondary - abstract via search summary]. Four lab experiments: after recalling a failure or weakness, self-compassion groups studied longer after a failed test (Exp 3), were more motivated to make amends for a moral transgression (Exp 2), and more motivated to change a weakness (Exp 4), compared with self-esteem and control groups.
- **Addiction evidence:** Berg et al. 2024, _Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy_ 31(3). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11360266/ [opened]: 18 studies of self-compassion and alcohol; higher self-compassion goes with less drinking (r about -0.13 to -0.28), mostly cross-sectional; only two treatment studies, mixed. "Suggestive but not yet conclusive." Self-forgiveness has been studied more in treatment.
- **Strength:** Moderate in the lab (motivation after failure), Weak in addiction. No porn data found. I did not verify Reid and colleagues' 2014 work on shame, rumination and self-compassion in treatment-seeking hypersexual men (a search did not return it).

# 2. Implementation intentions, habits, friction

### If-then plans

- **Claim:** "If situation X, then I will do Y" helps people act on goals, with a medium-to-large average effect in the published literature.
- **Sources:**
  - Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, _Advances in Experimental Social Psychology_ 38: 94 independent tests, d = .65. https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f [opened - abstract]
  - Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer, 2024 online / 2025 print, _European Review of Social Psychology_ 36(1), 162-194, DOI 10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563: 642 tests; effects on cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes ranged d = .27 to .66; plans did better when explicit and contingent, when motivation was high and when rehearsed. I could not open the page (403); this is from search summaries. [secondary]
  - Webb & Sheeran 2008, _British Journal of Social Psychology_: in 66 tests plans did not change goal intentions or self-efficacy, so plans do not work mainly by raising motivation. [secondary]
- **Strength:** Strong for health behaviour in general (meta-analyses), but see below for quitting.

### In substance use and for stopping behaviours

- **Claim:** For stopping or cutting down, effects are small and fade, and the evidence is weak in quality.
- **Sources:**
  - Malaguti et al. 2020, _Drug and Alcohol Dependence_. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32622228/ [opened]. 21 RCTs. Alcohol g = 0.31 (95% CI 0.21-0.42); tobacco g = 0.31 (0.12-0.50); no illicit-drug studies. "Small."
  - Cooke, McEwan & Norman 2022, _Drug and Alcohol Review_. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10087331/ [opened]. 16 studies, all rated high risk of bias. Weekly drinking d+ = -0.14 (-0.24 to -0.03); heavy episodic drinking d+ = -0.01 (null). Larger effects in community samples (-0.38) than university (-0.04), paper delivery (-0.26) than online (-0.04), and "volitional help sheets" (-0.34) than standard plans (-0.07). Effects shrank with longer follow-up.
  - A search summary mentioned a meta-analysis of 80 studies where plans aimed at reducing a behaviour had smaller effects (d = .23) than plans for promoting one (d = .42). I could not identify or open that source. Treat as a lead only. [secondary, unattributed]
- **Strength:** Weak-to-Moderate. No porn trial. The Hands-off program's "identify risky situations" step is the closest.

### What makes them work or fail

- **One plan beats several.** Verhoeven, Adriaanse et al. 2013, _European Journal of Social Psychology_: 63 young women (study 1) and 93 men and women (study 2); one if-then plan reduced snacking (2.01 to 1.47 a day) but three plans did not (1.95 to 1.83); short follow-up (3 days); the explanation (plans compete in memory) was not directly tested. https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/less-more-when-it-comes-beating-bad-habits [opened - a digest, not the paper]. Strength: Weak.
- **Do not write the plan as "if X, I will not Y."** I saw a reference to Adriaanse et al. 2011, "Planning what not to eat," reporting ironic effects of negation plans, but could not read its results. Lean on the standard advice (name a replacement action) as a design inference, not a proven finding. [secondary/unverified]
- **Low motivation and conflicting goals reduce benefits.** The 2024 meta-analysis reportedly found plans work better with high motivation [secondary]. Practitioner sources say half-hearted goals are poor candidates. A "coping plan" (if the plan fails or the barrier appears, then...) is associated with Sniehotta's work; I could not verify its evidence.
- **Format matters (alcohol review):** worksheet-style plans on paper outperformed brief online prompts. A daily lesson that makes the person write the plan may beat a tap-through.

### Habit formation

- **Claim:** New behaviours become automatic gradually and unevenly; one missed day barely matters.
- **Source:** Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle 2010, _European Journal of Social Psychology_ 40(6), 998-1009, via the BPS digest https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit [opened, digest] and a behavioural-science write-up [secondary]. 96 volunteers chose a daily eating, drinking or exercise behaviour; 82 completed. Median time to plateau 66 days, range 18 to a modelled 254. About half had not reached a habit-level score by day 84. A single missed day had little effect, but many missed days lowered the plateau. The 66-day figure rests on the 39 people whose data fit the curve.
- **Strength:** Weak. A small study of adding easy behaviours, not stopping a cue-driven one. "21 days" and "66 days to quit" are not supported.
- **A caution about the "no reset" finding:** use it to support "missing a day does not erase progress," not to claim a precise time to freedom.

### Stimulus control, friction and situational strategies versus willpower

- **Claim:** Changing the situation (avoiding, removing or making the cue harder) is theoretically better than resisting at the moment of temptation, but this has not been tested head to head.
- **Sources:**
  - Duckworth, Gendler & Gross 2016, _Perspectives on Psychological Science_ 11(1), 35-55. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736542/ [opened]. A theoretical review. The authors say no experiment has compared situational strategies directly with in-the-moment strategies; much of the support is correlational (people with high self-control meet fewer temptations), with some experiments in eating and studying and policy studies (default enrolment).
  - Hollands et al. 2019, Cochrane Review CD012573. https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3/information [secondary]. 24 studies, all on food; none on alcohol or tobacco. Moving food farther away: SMD -0.60 for consumption (95% CI -0.84 to -0.36), low certainty. Fewer options reduced selection but consumption effect was uncertain. 20 of 24 had risk-of-bias concerns, and some studies were co-authored by a researcher with retracted papers.
  - Wood and Rünger 2016, _Annual Review of Psychology_ 67, 289-314, and Wood & Neal reviews on habit change through context disruption: I saw the references but did not read them. [secondary]
- **Strength:** Expert view plus low-certainty experiments (food). No porn data. The cheap actions (filters, devices out of bedroom, changing the evening route) are worth teaching, but say plainly that there are no trials for porn.

# 3. Identity

### "Identity shifts" in behaviour change

- **Claim:** In qualitative studies, lasting change often follows a turning-point shift in how a person sees themselves.
- **Source I could verify:** a 2004 _Evidence-Based Nursing_ review of qualitative reports of turning points and processes of health behaviour change: lasting changes began with critical self-appraisal and small steps that led to an identity shift. https://ebn.bmj.com/content/7/1/31 [secondary - the page did not load for me]
- **Correction to the brief:** the author pair I found is Kearney and O'Sullivan (2003, _Western Journal of Nursing Research_), not Kearney and O'Brien. I could not find or open the original paper.
- **Strength:** Weak (qualitative synthesis).

### Social identity and recovery

- **Claim:** Recovery involves leaving a "user" social identity and building a "recovery" identity through group membership.
- **Source:** Best, Beckwith, Haslam et al. 2016, _Addiction Research & Theory_, Social Identity Model of Recovery (SIMOR). https://shura.shu.ac.uk/10842/ [secondary - the page was blocked; described in search summaries as a theoretical paper]. Related: Dingle, Cruwys & Frings (qualitative, two identity pathways: losing a positive identity vs gaining a "user" identity); Mawson, Best et al. pilot linking identification with non-using groups to lower use.
- **Strength:** Weak/Expert view. It points toward belonging, not just self-talk.

### Identity-based motivation (Oyserman)

- **Claim:** When an action feels like "something people like me do," difficulty reads as a sign that the task matters; when it does not feel identity-congruent, the same difficulty reads as "this isn't for me."
- **Source:** Oyserman identity-based motivation papers (2007, 2009, 2024 review in _Social and Personality Psychology Compass_). https://dornsife.usc.edu/daphna-oyserman/wp-content/uploads/sites/232/2024/12/Social-Personality-Psych-2024-Oyserman-Identity‐Based-Motivation-and-the-Motivational-Consequences-of-Difficulty.pdf [secondary - search summary only]
- **Strength:** Moderate in lab and school settings; not tested for addiction or porn. Design relevance: it supports presenting urges and hard days as a normal part of someone changing, rather than as evidence the person is "still an addict."

### "Be a voter" (the noun trick)

- **Claim:** Phrasing a goal as a noun about the self ("be a voter," implicitly "be a clean person") will raise follow-through.
- **Sources:** Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck 2011, _PNAS_ (original: 11 to 14 point turnout rise in two US elections). Failed to replicate: Gerber, Huber, Biggers & Hendry 2016, _PNAS_ (approximately zero difference); Fang, Gerber & Huber (_Political Psychology_) and a 2023 preregistered replication in a high-profile presidential election, _Behavioural Public Policy_, also null. The original authors disputed that the replication was authentic. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087036 [opened - this is Bryan's reply letter]; null findings from search summaries at https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps16-06 and https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/abs/voting-behavior-is-unaffected-by-subtle-linguistic-cues-evidence-from-a-psychologically-authentic-replication/7CA9BE69C3213D5030A2BD11A5C53450 [secondary]
- **Strength:** Contested/Weak. **Do not build a lesson on "say I am, not I do."** The idea of identity as a direction is fine; the wording trick is not supported.

### Self-affirmation and values work

- **Claim:** Writing about your core values lowers defensiveness and makes behaviour change slightly more likely.
- **Source:** Epton, Harris, Kane, van Koningsbruggen & Sheeran 2015, _Health Psychology_ 34(3), 187-196: 144 tests; effect on message acceptance d+ about 0.17, intentions about 0.14, behaviour about 0.32 (95% CI about 0.19-0.44). Stronger when the health risk was near. https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/the-impact-of-self-affirmation-on-health-behavior-change-a-meta-a/ [secondary - numbers from the search summary]
- **Strength:** Moderate for small effects in health behaviour; no data on porn.
- **Values in ACT:** the porn ACT trials above are values-driven, and the 2010 pilot showed psychological flexibility rising as viewing fell. Weak.

### The label problem

- Grubbs et al. 2015 (via a news summary): perceived addiction, not amount of viewing, tracked distress. Grubbs et al. 2018: self-reported addiction predicted baseline use but not later use. I did not find an experiment testing whether adopting the label "addict" helps or harms. [secondary] Strength of "label harms": Weak/Expert view.
- Design implication: choose words that give a direction ("someone who is building a life without this") rather than a diagnosis, and never make identity depend on an unbroken run (see streaks).

# 4. What predicts lasting recovery in PPU

**The honest answer: there is no good long-term prospective study of what predicts staying free of porn.** What exists is mostly cross-sectional or short-term, and mostly predicts who reports problems, not who recovers.

### Motives: stress, emotional avoidance, boredom

- **Claim:** People who use porn to escape stress or difficult feelings are more likely to have problematic use.
- **Sources:** Bőthe et al. 2021, _Psychology of Addictive Behaviors_ 35(2), 172-186, the Pornography Use Motivations Scale: stress reduction, emotional distraction/suppression, boredom avoidance, fantasy and sexual pleasure motives were associated with problematic use, weak-to-moderate. A 16-country machine-learning study (700+ variables) reported top predictors of PPU as frequency, emotional avoidance, stress reduction, moral incongruence and sexual shame; I could not open the paper and have not identified the full citation. [secondary]
- **Design:** cross-sectional surveys. **Strength:** Weak (cross-sectional), though consistent across studies.

### Daily-life data

- Henry et al. 2025, _Archives of Sexual Behavior_ (22 people, 4-week EMA): moods and thoughts shift before and after porn and masturbation; common self-reported triggers were loneliness, stress and social media; high moral-incongruence people showed a rebound pattern. A 2025 _Journal of Behavioral Addictions_ daily-diary study of US men found that the amount of use on a given day was the strongest correlate of control problems, and baseline measures did not predict the next 14 days well. [secondary]
- **Strength:** Weak (small, exploratory).

### Loneliness

- Butler et al. 2017/2018, _Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy_ 44(2), 127-137: 1,247 online respondents, positive association between porn use and loneliness in both directions. A later narrative review says longitudinal studies are unclear. https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/4470 [secondary]. **Strength:** Weak.

### Shame and guilt

- Gilliland, South, Carpenter & Hardy 2011, _Sexual Addiction & Compulsivity_ 18(1), 12-29 (from a BYU thesis of 177 treatment-seeking people): shame-proneness related to more hypersexuality; guilt-proneness related to motivation to change. [secondary]
- Randles & Tracy 2013, _Clinical Psychological Science_ (newly sober AA members; displayed shame on video predicted relapse, self-reported shame and guilt did not). [secondary] These are alcohol, small sample, shame-from-body-language.
- Treatment-seeking men: Reid and colleagues found self-critical emotion and rumination related to hypersexual behavior; I could not retrieve the paper. [unverified]
- **Strength:** Weak-to-Moderate; direction of causation unproven; remember Kirchner (section 1B) found self-blame not harmful after lapses in smoking.

### Abstinence effort and "perceived addiction"

- Fernandez, Tee & Fernandez 2017, _Sexual Addiction & Compulsivity_ 24(3), 156-179. https://tandfonline.com/doi/10.1080/10720162.2017.1344166 [secondary - page blocked]. 76 men told to abstain from internet porn for 14 days and log failed attempts. Perceived compulsivity scores were higher with more abstinence effort and with more failed attempts when effort was high; moral disapproval predicted emotional distress but not perceived compulsivity.
- **Read:** Trying hard to quit and repeatedly failing may drive up the feeling of compulsion, and moral disapproval drives distress, not "compulsion." **Strength:** Weak (one study, 76 men).

### Disclosure, accountability and social connection

- **For alcohol:** Kelly, Humphreys & Ferri 2020 Cochrane Review of AA and 12-step facilitation: found similar benefit to other treatments on most drinking outcomes and better results for continuous abstinence and remission; one study showed 42% vs 35% abstinent at 12 months (RR 1.2). A commentator judged the abstinence advantage not solid. https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012880.pub2/information [secondary]. **Strength:** Moderate for alcohol.
- **For compulsive sexual behavior:** Efrati & Gola 2018, _Journal of Behavioral Addictions_: members further along in a 12-step program had lower CSB and higher well-being (cross-sectional self-report). No RCTs of Sex Addicts Anonymous or similar found. [secondary] **Strength:** Weak.
- **Disclosure and accountability partners:** I found no study of telling a partner or friend, or of accountability software, on porn outcomes. **Expert view only.**
- **Forum journals:** Fernandez, Kuss & Griffiths 2021 (104 abstinence journals on Reboot Nation). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7889567/ [opened]. Members said internal strategies and social support helped; more than half reported at least one relapse; positive-outcome bias likely. **Strength:** Weak.

### Jay Stringer, _Unwanted_

- **What it is:** A survey of "nearly 4,000" (other sources say over 3,800) men and women, about 100 questions on family of origin, early events, current behaviours and "arousal templates." Stringer says unwanted sexual behaviour is not random and links to unaddressed parts of a person's story, including childhood sexual abuse and shame.
- **Source:** The Allender Center description https://theallendercenter.org/?p=8876 [opened]; podcast and publisher pages [secondary]. These pages gave no percentages, no recruitment method and no sign of peer review.
- **Evidence level:** Expert view only. A non-peer-reviewed practitioner survey; recruitment unknown. Do not cite numbers from it. Its idea (look at the person's story and unmet needs, not only the behaviour) fits the motive and shame findings above, but does not add independent proof.

# 5. Moral incongruence (the critical section for a faith-leaning app)

### The model and the meta-analysis

- **Claim:** How addicted a person feels to porn is predicted more by moral disapproval of it, and by religiosity through that disapproval, than by how much they watch.
- **Source:** Grubbs, Perry, Wilt & Reid 2019, "Pornography problems due to moral incongruence: an integrative model with a systematic review and meta-analysis," _Archives of Sexual Behavior_ 48(2), 397-415. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30076491/ [opened - abstract has no numbers]. Numbers as reported by Jennings et al. 2021 (_Journal of Behavioral Addictions_ 10(4), 854-878, a systematic review of 46 articles/59 studies, mostly cross-sectional, mostly White heterosexual): religiosity to self-perceived problems r = 0.31; religiosity to moral incongruence r = 0.65; moral incongruence to self-perceived problems r = 0.67. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8987424 [opened]
- **Other findings in Jennings:** 46 of 59 studies found a significant religiosity-CSB relation; most were positive but small in direct form; self-perceived porn addiction agreement ran 9-18% in men and 3-5% in women across five studies, and religiousness predicted it in all five.
- **Strength:** Moderate (many studies, large samples; mostly cross-sectional).

### Individual studies

- Grubbs, Exline, Pargament, Hook & Carlisle 2015, _Archives of Sexual Behavior_ 44, 125-136, three studies including undergraduates (n=331 and n=97): stronger faith linked to more moral disapproval, which linked to more perceived addiction; actual use did not predict perceived addiction the same way; religiosity unrelated to actual use levels among users. https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10508-013-0257-z [secondary]
- Grubbs, Kraus & Perry 2019, _Journal of Behavioral Addictions_ 8(1), 88-93: nationally representative US sample, N = 2,075; about 11% of men and 3% of women agreed they were addicted to porn; moral incongruence, religiousness, male sex, younger age and greater use were the strongest correlates. [secondary]
- Grubbs, Lee, Hoagland, Kraus & Perry 2020, _Clinical Psychological Science_: N = 2,519 (1,424 past-year users); religiousness was negatively related to use, but among more religious people use was more strongly tied to self-reported addiction. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/clinical/2167702620922966/ [opened - abstract]
- Grubbs, Kraus, Perry, Lewczuk & Gola 2020, _Journal of Abnormal Psychology_ 129(3), 266-278: two studies, over 3,500 people; Study 2 followed 850 US adult porn users every 4 months for a year: increases in moral disapproval tracked increases in feeling addicted. The authors suggest clinicians look at objective markers (failed attempts to cut down, impairment at work or home) rather than self-report alone, and say they do not suggest people change their moral or religious beliefs. https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2020/02/religious-moral-porn-addiction [opened - press release]
- Grubbs, Wilt, Exline & Pargament 2018, _Addictive Behaviors_ 82, 57-64 (two longitudinal samples, N = 3,988 and 1,047): perceived addiction predicted higher use at baseline but did not predict later use. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29494859/ [secondary - abstract via search summary]
- Lewczuk et al. 2020 (as reported in Jennings): religiosity was not correlated with self-perceived addiction in that Polish sample, but correlated with moral disapproval (r = .44) and moral-incongruence distress (r = .22). Not every sample reproduces the pattern. [opened, via Jennings]

### What these results do and do not mean (my reading, flagged as interpretation)

1. **Feeling addicted is not the same as being dysregulated.** Perceived addiction does not forecast later use (Grubbs 2018). Therefore a quiz that asks "do you feel addicted?" is a poor way to decide who has a problem, and it will flag religious people more.
2. **The distress is still real.** Perceived addiction is associated with depression, anxiety and stress, small but significant, in press summaries of Grubbs et al. 2015 (_Psychology of Addictive Behaviors_) [secondary]. A similar pattern with depression is reported for people who disapprove of porn (a Perry paper, 2018, _Society and Mental Health_), but I could not confirm it. Some papers talk of religiosity "inflating" the self-reports; for the app, treat the pain as real and the label as unreliable.
3. **Objective markers are better.** Failed attempts to cut down, impairment, time lost. Daybook's log can ask about these without asking whether the person "is addicted."
4. **Fernandez 2017** (section 4) suggests moral disapproval raises distress, whereas effort-and-failure raises perceived compulsivity. The two can be separated and need different help.

### Treatment among religious people

- The one RCT (Crosby & Twohig 2016) was almost entirely Latter-day Saint men, and ACT helped them without trying to change their beliefs. Strength: Weak-to-Moderate.
- The Hands-off web program (section 1A) found moral incongruence did not change even as problems fell.
- A 2025 latent-profile study mentioned in search results (42 countries, N about 67,000) reportedly found the religious moral-incongruence group did not show heightened distress or treatment seeking compared with groups with high problematic use. I could not open it; treat as a lead. [secondary]
- I found no trial of a program designed specifically for moral incongruence, and nothing on self-forgiveness or religious-coping interventions for porn (the search quota ran out before I could look). **Evidence gap: stated plainly in the lessons.**

### What reduces harm (inference from the above, not from a trial)

- Do not add moral argument. The app should neither affirm nor attack the person's beliefs; it should help them act on what they want.
- Keep "I don't want this because of my values" separate from "I can't stop." They respond to different help.
- Use ACT-style willingness and values language (works in religious men, targets shame).
- Treat setbacks without verdict words: sin, fall, impure, broken, addict, relapse. Those words strengthen moral incongruence, the strongest predictor of feeling addicted. This is my inference from the predictor data; it has not been tested.
- Report progress with objective, non-moral measures (hard hours survived, urges ridden out, days with no use), not "purity."
- Offer a short sentence for people who find their distress comes mostly from the values conflict: this is common, it is real, and it can be named without deciding anything. (Design suggestion, untested.)

# 6. What goes wrong, and which popular claims are not supported

### Shame

- Associated with more problem behaviour in treatment-seeking and recovering samples (section 4), and reduced by ACT in a shame-focused RCT, with better attendance and fewer days of use afterward (Luoma 2012). But the best prospective lapse study found no harm from self-blame (Kirchner 2012). **Verdict:** shame is unhelpful as a motivator, and likely harmful for engagement, but "shame causes relapse" is not firmly established. Weak-to-Moderate.

### Willpower framing

- No experiment compares willpower with situational control (Duckworth 2016). Hands-off found avoidance self-efficacy rose with the program (d = 0.87). Self-efficacy has real predictive value for smoking relapse (d = -.21 before quit day, -.47 after the quit day, per Hendershot 2011). **Verdict:** confidence helps; "just resist harder" has no evidence behind it.

### Abstinence violation effect and "I ruined everything"

- See section 1B. The effect is not as firmly tested as its fame suggests, but lapses cluster, big lapses lead to more lapses, and low confidence after a lapse predicts faster return. The all-or-nothing stance deserves removal either way.

### Ironic suppression (Wegner)

- **Claim:** Trying not to think of something makes it return, but the average rebound is small.
- **Sources:** Magee, Harden & Teachman 2012, _Clinical Psychology Review_ 32(3), 189-201 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3307846 [opened]): during suppression, people did suppress (d+ = -.36); after suppression, thoughts rebounded slightly (d+ = .15, 95% CI .04-.27). Clinical and non-clinical groups did not differ. Wang, Hagger & Chatzisarantis 2020, _Perspectives on Psychological Science_ 15(3) (https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/60795/ [opened - abstract]): 31 studies; rebound occurred regardless of cognitive load; enhancement during suppression appeared only under load; I could not get the effect size. Abramowitz, Tolin & Street 2001 meta-analysis (28 controlled studies, small-to-moderate rebound). [secondary]
- **Sexual thoughts:** correlational study of 661 Israeli teens (Efrati) linked suppression to more preoccupation; small lab studies suggest a rebound for sexual words. I found no study of suppressing porn urges. [secondary]
- **Strength:** Moderate that rebound exists, but it is small. **Do not say "suppression always backfires."** Say "fighting a thought tends to keep it close, and there is a small rebound; making room for it works at least as well."

### Streak culture

- Silverman & Barasch 2023, _Journal of Consumer Research_ 49(6), 1095-1117. https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb [secondary]. Seven studies: an intact streak raises later engagement versus a broken one, because people treat keeping the streak as the goal; stronger when people blame themselves for the break; some people pay or watch ads to repair the streak. The authors suggest avoiding "you broke your streak" messaging and offering repair options.
- **Strength:** Moderate for the mechanism (experiments); none in addiction. Fits the owner's decision to avoid all-or-nothing counts, but note that on strict, a slip puts the count to 0; the research suggests this will hit self-blaming users hardest.

### Overstated brain claims

- Kraus, Voon & Potenza 2016, _Addiction_ 111(12), 2097-2106. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26893127/ [opened]: CSB shares features with substance addictions (craving, attentional bias, treatment approaches), but "significant gaps in understanding continue to complicate classification."

[secondary]
- A 2025 striatal review reported heightened striatal responses to sexual cues in PPU/CSBD, and Gola et al. 2017 reported a stronger reward response to cues that predict erotic images in treatment-seeking men. A 2025 preprint protocol states no coordinate-based meta-analysis existed. [secondary]
- I could not open Kühn & Gallinat 2014 (_JAMA Psychiatry_) and so cannot describe it.
- **Verdict:** Brain-scan findings are small-sample, correlational, and contested. "Porn shrinks your brain / hijacks dopamine for months" is not established. What is on firmer ground is that craving and cue reactivity exist, and that urges pass without acting on them. Do not use neuroscience as the hook.

# 7. What a person could be taught to DO

Order is how I would teach it. "Evidence" gives strength for the skill itself and where it comes from. Skills 1 to 5 are the first two weeks; the owner's rule that big goals wait until a week of no slips is compatible with this order.

| #   | Skill                                                      | What the person does                                                                                                                                                                                       | Evidence strength                                                    | Based on                                                                                                                                                                    |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Say what you want and why, in your own words**           | Write one or two sentences on the life they want and what the habit costs, without borrowing a verdict from anyone.                                                                                        | Weak-to-Moderate                                                     | ACT values work (Crosby & Twohig 2016; pilot with rising psychological flexibility); self-affirmation d+ about .32 on behaviour (Epton 2015); Hands-off "motivation" module |
| 2   | **Keep an honest log without verdicts**                    | Record urge, slip, time, place, feeling, what was going on. Look at it weekly.                                                                                                                             | Weak (porn); Expert view                                             | Hands-off daily diary; Kraus self-initiated strategies (recording time) [unverified]; standard CBT                                                                          |
| 3   | **Learn your own pattern: times, places, feelings, needs** | Name top 3 risky situations: tired and alone at night, stress after work, bored with phone in bed. Name the job the habit does (escape, soothe, switch off, company, boredom).                             | Weak-to-Moderate                                                     | Hands-off "risky situations"; Bőthe 2021 motives; EMA triggers of loneliness, stress; RP model                                                                              |
| 4   | **Change the setting first**                               | Move the phone charger out of the bedroom; filters; no device in the bathroom; change evening route. Make the first step harder.                                                                           | Expert view plus low-certainty experiments on food; no porn trials   | Duckworth 2016; Hollands 2019 (food proximity SMD -0.60)                                                                                                                    |
| 5   | **Make one if-then plan with a replacement**               | "If I am on my phone in bed after 10 pm and feel the pull, then I put the phone on the charger and do [specific thing]." One plan, rehearsed, on paper. Add a second plan only after the first is working. | Moderate (health behaviour); Weak (quitting); none for porn          | Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006; Malaguti 2020; Cooke 2022 (paper beat online); Verhoeven 2013 (one beats three)                                                                  |
| 6   | **Ride the urge (name it, watch it, let it pass)**         | Notice the urge, name the feeling and body sensation, breathe, wait it out without acting; practise on small urges first. Say plainly that the urge may not shrink; the aim is to act differently anyway.  | Weak (urge surfing); Weak-to-Moderate (mindfulness/ACT in addiction) | Bowen & Marlatt 2009; Elwafi 2013; Grant 2017 (craving SMD -0.13); Cochrane 2021; ACT                                                                                       |
| 7   | **Have something else that meets the need**                | A short menu matched to the job from skill 3: stress (movement, shower, breathing), loneliness (message a person), boredom (a defined activity), tiredness (go to sleep).                                  | Weak                                                                 | Bőthe motives; Hands-off "enjoyable activities" module; no direct test of which substitutes work                                                                            |
| 8   | **Plan for the slip before it happens**                    | A 3-line "after a slip" plan: what I tell myself, what I do in the first hour, who or what helps. Includes closing the gap quickly and not "using up" the rest of the day.                                 | Moderate (smoking); Weak (porn)                                      | Kirchner 2012 (confidence and lapse size matter); Hendershot 2011; Hands-off relapse prevention module                                                                      |
| 9   | **Treat yourself like a friend after a lapse**             | Write what a friend would be told. Name what the slip taught.                                                                                                                                              | Moderate (lab motivation); Weak (addiction)                          | Breines & Chen 2012; Berg 2024; Luoma 2012                                                                                                                                  |
| 10  | **Defuse harsh thoughts instead of arguing with them**     | "I am having the thought that I'm hopeless." Notice, thank the mind, do the next small thing.                                                                                                              | Weak-to-Moderate                                                     | ACT trials (Crosby & Twohig 2016); small suppression rebound (Magee 2012 d+ .15)                                                                                            |
| 11  | **Build confidence from evidence**                         | Keep a "proof" list: urges ridden out, nights handled, the longest runs and comebacks.                                                                                                                     | Moderate (smoking self-efficacy)                                     | Hendershot 2011 (d = -.21 / -.47); Kirchner 2012; Hands-off self-efficacy d = 0.87                                                                                          |
| 12  | **Take one valued action a day, urge or no urge**          | A small action toward the life in skill 1 (call, walk, sleep on time, finish a task).                                                                                                                      | Weak-to-Moderate                                                     | ACT "committed action"; Hands-off habits and activities                                                                                                                     |
| 13  | **Reach out to one person (optional, and gradual)**        | Pick one safe person or group; the app offers a script for a first sentence. Not required.                                                                                                                 | Weak (porn); Moderate (alcohol peer groups)                          | Kelly 2020 Cochrane (AA/TSF); Efrati & Gola 2018; SIMOR theory. No evidence on disclosure to partners; do not promise results.                                              |
| 14  | **Separate your values from your shame**                   | If the person holds a moral or religious view of porn, learn to tell apart "this goes against what I value" from "I am bad." Both can be true; only the second is a verdict.                               | Weak (inference)                                                     | Moral incongruence data (Grubbs 2019, 2020); ACT with religious men; Luoma 2012                                                                                             |
| 15  | **See yourself as someone changing, with proof**           | Frame identity as a direction backed by the log (not as a status that a slip removes). Hard days read as "this matters to me."                                                                             | Weak                                                                 | Oyserman; SIMOR; Kearney; the noun trick does not replicate                                                                                                                 |
| 16  | **Let it become ordinary**                                 | Expect the new routine to feel more automatic over weeks to months, with a wide range; a missed day does not erase progress.                                                                               | Weak                                                                 | Lally 2010                                                                                                                                                                  |

**Where the order comes from.** Skills 1 to 3 give the person a reason, a record and a map, and cost nothing. Skill 4 (friction) is cheap and works before any urge appears. Skill 5 gives a rehearsed response. Skill 6 comes once they have a map so they know what they are riding. Skills 8 and 9 should be taught before the first slip, not after: after a slip people are least able to learn them. Skills 13 to 16 are about staying free long term and fit better after a week or more.

**What I would NOT teach as fact**

- A number of days to "rewire the brain."
- That suppression always backfires.
- That shame causes relapse (say shame gets in the way of staying engaged and of reaching out).
- That the person is or is not an "addict," or that they should or should not feel their porn use is wrong.
- That the noun phrasing of identity changes behaviour.
- That a streak is something to protect.
- That erectile problems are caused by porn, or that "flatline" is a normal stage.
- "Willpower is a muscle." I did not research this, so I make no claim.
